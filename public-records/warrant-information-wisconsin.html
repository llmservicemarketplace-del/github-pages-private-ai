Check Warrant Information in Wisconsin: Official Court and Law-Enforcement Sources

Last reviewed November 12, 2026. Written by The New Faith Church and Co-op, Inc. (NFCcop), a 501(c)(3) nonprofit educational resource. General information only, not legal advice. We sell no legal, search, background-check, or filing services.

If you think there may be a warrant with your name on it, the safest step is to contact the court or a lawyer, not to call around or ask someone else to check for you. This page explains where official Wisconsin records are kept, what the different warrant types mean, and why an online "no result" can be wrong.

This page is for people checking their own status or an official case record. It does not list names, photos, or addresses of anyone with an alleged warrant, and it does not help anyone avoid law enforcement.

Short answer: Wisconsin does not publish a single statewide public warrant-search tool that we could verify. Warrant records are held by the court that issued the warrant and by the county sheriff or city police. Contact the court clerk directly for your own case.

We could not verify a statewide, official warrant search for Wisconsin. That is normal. Warrants are issued by judges in local cases and stored by the court and the arresting agency. Some counties publish a jail roster or a wanted list; many do not publish warrants at all. A commercial "warrant search" site is not an official source and cannot confirm whether a warrant exists.

Warrant records are usually kept locally

A warrant is a court order. It is issued by a judge in a specific case, in a specific county or city. The court clerk keeps the case file. The sheriff or police department keeps the arrest record. There is often no single office that can see every warrant in the state.

That means the county matters. If you were arrested, cited, or told to appear in a particular court, start there. If you do not know the county, work backward from where the event happened, where you lived, or where the case was filed.

How to identify the county that may hold the record

  • Where were you arrested or cited? The arresting agency and the local court are the first places to check.
  • Where did the case happen? A charge is usually filed in the county where the conduct allegedly occurred.
  • Where do you live? A failure-to-appear warrant is usually issued by the court you missed, not by the court near your home.
  • Do you have a case number? Any letter, citation, or booking sheet can point to the right clerk.

If you are unsure, call the clerk of the court you think is involved and ask how to check your own case. Write down the name of the person you speak with and the date.

Four kinds of warrants, and what they mean

TypeWhat it usually means
Arrest warrantA judge has authorized police to arrest a person based on probable cause in a criminal case.
Bench warrantA judge issues it from the bench, often for missing a court date, failing to comply with an order, or violating a condition of release.
Failure-to-appear warrantA warrant issued after a person does not show up for a required hearing. It may be called a bench warrant or an FTA warrant.
Search warrantPermission to search a place or seize property. It is not an arrest warrant, and it is often sealed while the investigation is active.

A "wanted" notice, a jail roster, and a warrant are different things. A person can appear on a jail roster without an open warrant, and a warrant can exist without any public listing.

What online records can and cannot tell you

  • Not every warrant is public. Sealed cases, juvenile matters, and active investigations may be hidden by law.
  • Information can be delayed. A warrant may be issued, recalled, or served before a website updates.
  • Records can be incomplete. Small agencies may not publish anything online.
  • Records can be removed. A dismissed case or recalled warrant may disappear, or may linger after it is no longer active.
  • A "no result" is not proof. A name may be misspelled, a middle name may be missing, or the record may sit in a different county.
  • Names can match the wrong person. Never confront or accuse someone based on a website result.
Online information may be out of date. Even an official court portal can lag. Confirm with the clerk or the issuing court before you act on anything you see.

Safer next steps if you think there is a warrant

  1. Contact the court clerk. Ask how to check your own case and whether a warrant is active. You do not have to explain your whole situation on the phone.
  2. Talk to a defense lawyer. A lawyer can check the docket, explain the warrant, and tell you about options such as appearing, recalling the warrant, or asking for a new date.
  3. Contact a public defender if you are eligible. Eligibility is based on income and the type of case. Ask the court or the local public defender's office.
  4. Contact legal aid. Legal aid usually does not defend criminal cases, but it can help with related housing, benefits, or family problems.
  5. Keep a written record. Note the court, case number, date, and who you spoke with. If you hire a lawyer, bring it to the first meeting.

Safety notice

Do not confront another person about an alleged warrant. Do not go to a home, workplace, or school to demand answers, and do not post someone's name or photo online. If you believe a person is dangerous, call 911. If you believe you have a warrant, contact the court or a lawyer rather than trying to resolve it informally.

This page does not tell anyone how to evade, hide from, or interfere with law enforcement. If there is an active warrant, the legal path is to appear, get advice, and ask the court about the options that apply to your case.

Common questions

Is there a statewide warrant search in Wisconsin?

No. We could not verify a statewide public warrant search for Wisconsin. Warrants are held by the issuing court and local law enforcement, so contact the county clerk or sheriff for your own case.

Can I check my own warrant status online?

Sometimes. A court case search may show a criminal case and a warrant, but coverage varies. The most reliable check is to call the clerk of the court handling your case and ask about your own record.

Does "no results" mean there is no warrant?

No. Records can be delayed, sealed, incomplete, or filed under a different name or county. Only the issuing court or agency can confirm whether a warrant is active.

Should I call the police to ask about a warrant?

You can ask the court clerk first. If you call a police department, use its published non-emergency number, not 911. Do not give personal information to a caller who contacts you demanding payment to "clear a warrant"; that is a common scam.
