How to Replace Medications Lost in a Disaster

Last reviewed November 9, 2026. Free public guidance from The New Faith Church and Co-op, Inc. (NFCcop), a 501(c)(3) nonprofit. This is general information, not legal or professional advice. Rules and deadlines can change, so confirm with the official sources below.

If your medicines were lost, left behind, or damaged by water or heat, you have options to get them replaced quickly, even if you evacuated far from home or don't have insurance.

Which medicines to throw out

  • Throw out pills, liquids, inhalers, and other medicines that touched floodwater or contaminated water, even if the bottle looks sealed.
  • Medicines exposed to high heat, fire, or smoke may not work as well. Ask a pharmacist.
  • Life-saving medicines: if a medicine you depend on was damaged and you can't get a replacement right away, keep using it only until you can, and talk with a pharmacist or doctor as soon as possible.
  • Insulin and refrigerated medicines: many insulin products can stay at room temperature for a limited time, often up to 28 days, but this varies by product. Check the package insert or ask a pharmacist.

Getting refills

  1. Call your pharmacy. National chains can usually transfer your prescription to any of their stores, including in another state.
  2. Ask about emergency refills. Many states allow pharmacists to give an emergency supply when they can't reach your doctor. During a declared emergency, some states allow more; in Florida, pharmacists can dispense up to a 30-day emergency supply of most non-Schedule II medicines.
  3. Ask about early refills. Some states, including Florida, require insurers to allow early refills during a declared state of emergency.
  4. Call your doctor's office or use telehealth to get a new prescription sent wherever you are.

No insurance? EPAP

After some major disasters, the federal government activates the Emergency Prescription Assistance Program (EPAP). It helps people without insurance in the declared area get up to a 30-day supply of lost or damaged prescriptions, and some medical equipment like walkers, wheelchairs, and glucose meters, at participating pharmacies at no cost. Call 855-793-7470 to see if EPAP is active for your disaster. People with Medicare, Medicaid, or private insurance aren't eligible and should use their own coverage.

Lost medical equipment

Medicare may cover replacement of medical equipment lost or destroyed in a disaster. Call the equipment supplier and Medicare. FEMA may also help with medical equipment costs not covered by insurance. See the FEMA application guide.

Prepare now

Keep a medicine list and a photo of your prescription labels on your phone, and an extra supply in your go bag if your doctor and insurance allow it. See medical equipment during an outage and dialysis disaster planning.

Frequently asked questions

Should I throw out medicine that got wet in a flood?

Yes, if it touched floodwater, even if the container looks sealed. If it's a life-saving medicine with no replacement yet, talk with a pharmacist right away.

What is EPAP?

The Emergency Prescription Assistance Program helps uninsured people in declared disaster areas get up to a 30-day supply of lost prescriptions and some medical equipment at no cost. Call 855-793-7470.

Can I get an early refill before a hurricane?

Often, yes. Some states, including Florida, require insurers to allow early refills when a state of emergency is declared. Ask your pharmacy.
