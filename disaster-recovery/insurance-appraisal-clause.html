The Insurance Appraisal Clause: Disputing How Much You're Paid

Last reviewed November 10, 2026. Free public guidance from The New Faith Church and Co-op, Inc. (NFCcop), a 501(c)(3) nonprofit. This is general information, not legal or professional advice. Rules and deadlines can change, so confirm with the official sources below.

Most homeowners policies include an appraisal clause. It's a way to settle a disagreement about how much the damage costs to fix, without going to court. It usually can't decide whether something is covered at all.

How appraisal works

  1. You or the insurer make a written demand for appraisal, following your policy's wording.
  2. Each side picks its own independent appraiser.
  3. The two appraisers choose a neutral umpire.
  4. The appraisers try to agree on the amount of loss. If they can't, the umpire decides. Agreement by any two of the three usually sets the amount.

What it costs

You usually pay your own appraiser and split the umpire's fee with the insurer. Ask appraisers for their fee in writing before you hire them. In Florida, a public adjuster's fee cap can affect whether they may also act as your appraiser.

When appraisal makes sense

SituationAppraisal a good fit?
The insurer agrees the storm damage is covered, but its estimate is far below your contractor'sUsually yes
The insurer says the damage isn't covered (for example, wear and tear or flood)Usually no. That's a coverage dispute
A small difference in dollarsMaybe not. Fees can eat up the gain

Before you demand appraisal

  • Read your policy's appraisal section. Wording and deadlines vary.
  • Get a detailed, line-item estimate from a licensed contractor.
  • Try a written reconsideration request and a free state complaint first. See claim denied or underpaid.
  • Ask whether appraisal affects any other rights, such as suing later. If a lot of money is at stake, talk to a lawyer first.

Frequently asked questions

What is insurance appraisal?

A process in most homeowners policies where each side picks an appraiser, and a neutral umpire helps settle disagreements about the amount of a covered loss.

Can appraisal decide if my damage is covered?

Usually not. Appraisal generally decides only the amount of loss. Disputes about whether something is covered may need a complaint, mediation, or a lawyer.

Who pays for appraisal?

Usually you pay your appraiser, the insurer pays theirs, and you split the umpire's fee. Check your policy.
