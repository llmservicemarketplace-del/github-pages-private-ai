Mold After a Flood or Leak

Last reviewed November 5, 2026. Free public guidance from The New Faith Church and Co-op, Inc. (NFCcop), a 501(c)(3) nonprofit. This is general information, not legal or professional advice. Rules and deadlines can change, so confirm with the official sources below.

Mold grows on anything that stays wet: drywall, wood, carpet, and furniture. After a flood or a leak, the goal is to remove moldy materials you cannot clean, clean what you can, and fix the moisture so the mold does not come back.

Start with the moisture

Mold cleanup does not last if the area stays wet. Stop the leak, remove standing water, and dry the area with fans and dehumidifiers. Wet materials should be dried within 24 to 48 hours whenever possible. See flood cleanup for drying steps.

Can I clean it myself? The 10-square-foot rule

EPA guidance treats about 10 square feet, a patch roughly 3 feet by 3 feet, as the size most people can clean themselves.

  • Smaller than 10 sq ft: usually a do-it-yourself job with the right gear.
  • Larger than 10 sq ft: consider a trained mold professional and follow EPA's guidance for larger areas.
  • Call a professional regardless of size if: the water was sewage or contaminated floodwater, mold is inside your heating and cooling system or ducts, or anyone in the home has asthma, allergies, or a weakened immune system.

Protect yourself

  • An N95 respirator, not a dust mask
  • Long rubber or nitrile gloves
  • Goggles without vent holes
  • Long sleeves and pants you can wash or throw away

Children, pregnant people, and people with breathing problems should stay away during cleanup.

How to clean mold safely

  1. Photograph it first for your insurance claim, including close-ups and a wide shot showing where it is.
  2. Remove porous materials that have mold growing on them, such as drywall, ceiling tiles, carpet, and insulation. Bag them before carrying them through the house.
  3. Scrub hard surfaces with detergent and water, then let them dry completely.
  4. Disinfect if you choose to. If you use bleach, use no more than 1 cup per gallon of water with fresh air moving through the room. Never mix bleach with ammonia or other cleaners.
  5. Do not paint or caulk over mold. It will keep growing underneath and peel the paint.

Hold off on running fans in a moldy room until you have gently cleaned the visible mold, so you do not blow spores through the house.

Signs the mold is coming back

  • A musty smell that returns after cleaning
  • New spots or stains, or bubbling paint
  • Humidity that stays high indoors. Try to keep it under about 50 to 60 percent.

Hiring a mold contractor

There is no federal certification for mold contractors. A few states license mold work; Florida and Texas both do, so check your state's licensing board. Ask for a written scope of work, proof of insurance, and how they will confirm the job is finished. Be cautious of a company that both tests for mold and then sells you the removal. Read how to hire a contractor after a storm before signing.

Mold and insurance

Many homeowners policies limit or exclude mold, but mold caused by a covered water loss may be included up to a set limit. Report the water damage quickly and keep records showing you acted fast to dry the home.

Frequently asked questions

How big a mold area can I clean myself?

EPA guidance treats about 10 square feet (roughly 3 feet by 3 feet) as the size most people can handle. Larger areas, sewage-contaminated areas, and mold in ducts are jobs for a professional.

Does bleach kill mold?

Bleach can disinfect hard, non-porous surfaces after cleaning, but it does not fix mold inside porous materials like drywall. Those materials usually need to be removed. Use no more than 1 cup of bleach per gallon of water.

Can I paint over mold?

No. Paint does not stop mold. Remove or clean the mold and fix the moisture first.
