How to Tarp a Storm-Damaged Roof

Following high winds, hail, or fallen branches, prompt temporary roof covering helps protect home interiors from rain and interior collapse. However, working on roofs presents severe personal safety risks. Review these safety principles, assess damage carefully from the ground, and hire insured roofing professionals whenever conditions present hazards.

CRITICAL LIFE SAFETY WARNING — WHEN NOT TO CLIMB ONTO A ROOF:

Falls from roofs cause fatal or catastrophic injuries every year. Do NOT climb onto a roof if any of the following conditions exist:
  • The roof surface is wet, slick, icy, or covered in loose storm debris.
  • Active rain, lightning, or high wind gusts are present.
  • The roof pitch is steep (greater than standard low slopes) and you lack OSHA-compliant fall-arrest harness systems.
  • The roof shows structural sagging, broken rafters, or cracked decking.
  • Downed or sagging utility lines are anywhere near the roof or gutters.
  • You lack secure ladders, slip-resistant footwear, and an assistant on the ground to stabilize equipment.
Y PrivateAI strongly recommends hiring a licensed, insured roofing professional or requesting help from trained volunteer disaster crews when available.

1. Assessing Damage from the Ground

  • Use binoculars to inspect ridge vents, valleys, missing shingles, and cracked decking from multiple vantage points on the ground.
  • Estimate the square footage of the damaged section to ensure adequate materials before starting.
  • Take wide-angle and close-up photographs of all exterior roof damage from the ground before any temporary tarp is placed.

2. General Principles for Securing a Tarp to a Roof

  • Overlap Over the Peak (Ridge): Water flows downward. If a tarp edge ends below the ridge on the damaged slope, rainwater can easily seep under the top edge. Where practical, temporary coverings should extend over the roof peak to the opposite slope.
  • Avoid Nailing Directly Through Raw Fabric: Driving nails straight through loose tarp fabric can result in tears during wind gusts. Professional contractors commonly sandwich tarp edges into 1x2 or 2x4 wood furring strips before fastening down into structural rafters.
  • Keep the Fabric Taut: Loose, flapping tarps catch wind easily and can pull free or create noise and stress on fasteners.
  • Cover Beyond the Damage: A temporary tarp should extend at least several feet past the damaged area on all sides to prevent lateral water intrusion.

3. Professional Assistance & Emergency Volunteer Teams

  • Licensed Roofing Contractors: Check credentials, verify active workers' compensation and general liability insurance, and obtain a written estimate.
  • Volunteer Disaster Crews: Following declared emergencies, volunteer organizations coordinated through 211 or Crisis Cleanup may provide trained volunteers to assist vulnerable homeowners with emergency tarping when resources permit.
  • Operation Blue Roof: If activated by FEMA and the U.S. Army Corps of Engineers in your disaster area, qualified primary residences may be eligible for free temporary fiber-reinforced roof sheeting.

4. Document Damage and Retain Receipts

Emergency mitigation expenses may be reimbursable depending on the policy, cause of loss, coverage, limits, documentation, and circumstances. Take clear photographs of the damage before and after tarp placement, and store all receipts for lumber, tarps, screws, and professional services.

Authoritative Safety Standards

  • OSHA Fall Protection Standards & Safety Guidance — Official workplace safety standards detailing fall arrest requirements and roof hazard management.
  • Ready.gov Severe Weather Preparedness & Safety — Federal safety guidelines for protecting property and responding after damaging storms.
