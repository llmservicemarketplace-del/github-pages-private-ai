Find Fallen Tree Removal Help

Severe winds and saturated soil can uproot mature trees, crushing roofs, blocking access driveways, and tangling with overhead utilities. Clearing fallen trees involves substantial physical hazards, including tensioned wood, electrical shock, and severe chainsaw injury. Review these essential safety guidelines before initiating any tree clearing.

Never touch, approach, or attempt to cut any tree, limb, or brush that is in contact with or near a downed utility line.
  • Assume all downed wires are fully energized and lethal, even if not humming, smoking, or sparking.
  • Trees, wet ground, and metal fences conduct electricity readily.
  • Maintain a minimum perimeter of at least 35 feet away from any downed wire and immediately contact your local electric utility and 911.

1. Assessing Tree Hazards and Access Roadways

  • Trapped Occupants: If a fallen tree has impacted an occupied room or caused structural collapse, call 911 immediately for emergency rescue personnel.
  • Blocked Public Roads: If trees block public roadways or prevent emergency access, notify your municipal or county road department. Public road crews prioritize clearing emergency thoroughfares first.
  • Trees on Residential Structures: Trees resting on roofs exert massive structural weight. Attempting to pull trunks off a roof using vehicles or winches can cause sudden wall or truss collapse. Hire a qualified arborist or crane operator to safely rig and lift the tree.

2. Volunteer Chainsaw Crews

  • Volunteer Relief Organizations: Groups such as Southern Baptist Disaster Relief, Samaritan's Purse, Team Rubicon, and regional disaster response organizations deploy trained volunteer sawyer crews to help clear fallen trees and limbs from primary residences.
  • Hotline Registration: Check 211 (visit 211.org) or the Crisis Cleanup hotline (call 844-965-1386 when activated) to place your property into the volunteer intake queue. Availability depends on volunteer capacity.

3. Hiring Tree Service Contractors Safely

  • Credential Verification: Look for arborists certified by the International Society of Arboriculture (ISA) or members of the Tree Care Industry Association (TCIA).
  • Insurance Confirmation: Tree removal involves dangerous work. Request a current Certificate of Insurance covering both general liability and workers' compensation, and contact the issuing agency to confirm the policy is active.
  • Clear Written Estimates: Ensure written agreements specify whether the scope includes hauling wood away, chipping branches, bucking wood to curbside lengths, or stump grinding.

4. Vital Chainsaw Safety Protocols (OSHA & CDC Rules)

  • Mandatory Protective Equipment: Wear cut-resistant chainsaw chaps, steel-toed boots, heavy work gloves, safety glasses, and hearing protection.
  • Beware of Springpoles and Tension: Limbs pinned beneath heavy trunks store immense tension. Cutting a tensioned limb can cause it to whip violently, causing severe injury. Make small relief cuts and stand well clear of the anticipated recoil path.
  • Never Cut Overhead: Avoid cutting branches above shoulder height, which dramatically increases the danger of saw kickback and loss of balance.
  • Maintain Clear Retreat Paths: Before beginning cuts on large logs or leaning trees, clear two unobstructed paths diagonally away from the expected direction of fall.

Authoritative Tree Care & Power Line Safety Resources

  • OSHA Chainsaw Safety Standards & Operating Guidance — Official occupational safety guidelines on chainsaw handling, PPE, and kickback prevention.
  • Electrical Safety Foundation (ESFI) Downed Power Lines Guidance — Critical public safety rules on identifying and maintaining safe perimeters around downed utility lines.
  • TreesAreGood (International Society of Arboriculture - ISA) — Verify certified arborists and review professional post-storm tree risk assessment criteria.
