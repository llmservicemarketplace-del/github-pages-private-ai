Can I use a free state mediation program for my insurance dispute?

Leer en español

Some states offer mediation for disputed residential property claims. A neutral mediator helps you and the insurer try to settle. In many programs the insurer pays the mediator's fee.

Mediation is voluntary to settle: you don't have to accept an offer. Programs and rules vary by state and sometimes only run after declared disasters.

The short answer. Ask your state insurance department whether it has a property claim mediation program and whether your claim qualifies. Bring your estimate, photos, and a clear number you'd accept. Rules differ by state and by policy wording.

What this turns on

  • Whether your state has a program and your claim qualifies.
  • Whether the dispute is about amount or coverage.
  • Your settlement range and evidence.

Examples

  • Florida: the Department of Financial Services runs a mediation program for residential property claims.
  • North Carolina: a disaster mediation program for disputed residential claims from declared disasters, generally $1,500 or more in dispute. The insurer pays program costs. It excludes NFIP flood policies and claims denied on policy exclusions.
  • Louisiana: mediation for residential claims up to $150,000 after a declared named-storm emergency, through firms listed by the Department of Insurance.

Other states have programs too. Check with your state insurance department.

How to prepare

  • A one-page summary of what's disputed and why.
  • Your estimate side by side with the insurer's. See comparing estimates.
  • Your lowest acceptable number, decided ahead of time.
  • Know what you give up if you sign a settlement and release.

What your file needs

  • Both estimates.
  • Photos and key documents.
  • A short dispute summary.

Quick answers

Who pays for state insurance mediation?

In many state programs, like North Carolina's disaster program, the insurer pays program costs. Check your state's rules.

Do I have to accept a settlement in mediation?

No. Mediation helps both sides try to reach agreement. You don't have to accept an offer, but read any release carefully before signing.
