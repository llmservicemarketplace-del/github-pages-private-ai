How do wind vs. flood disputes work after a hurricane?

Leer en español

Hurricanes bring both wind and water. Homeowners policies usually cover wind but exclude flood, while flood policies cover flood but not wind. That leaves homeowners in the middle when each side points to the other.

Many policies also contain anti-concurrent causation language, which can exclude damage when flood and a covered cause combine. How courts read it varies by state.

The short answer. Build a timeline and evidence for what damage the wind did before the water arrived: roof and window damage, interior water lines above flood level, debris patterns, and storm data. File under both policies if you have both, and keep the claims coordinated. Rules differ by state and by policy wording.

What this turns on

  • Which damage happened from wind and which from rising water.
  • Your policy's flood exclusion and causation wording.
  • Whether you have both homeowners and flood policies.

Evidence that separates wind from water

  • Roof damage, missing shingles, broken windows, and torn siding above the flood line.
  • Interior water stains from the ceiling down (wind-driven rain) vs. a high-water line from the ground up (flood).
  • Neighbors' damage, debris direction, and photos or video during the storm.
  • Storm data: wind speeds and storm surge timing near your address.

If you have both policies

  • File both claims promptly and tell each insurer about the other.
  • Keep separate damage lists for wind and flood.
  • Watch NFIP proof-of-loss deadlines. See NFIP proof of loss.

What your file needs

  • Photos showing roof and upper damage.
  • High-water line measurements.
  • Storm data for your location.
  • Both policies' declarations pages.

Quick answers

Does homeowners insurance cover hurricane flood damage?

Usually not. Homeowners policies generally cover wind damage and exclude flood. Flood requires a separate flood policy.

What is anti-concurrent causation?

Policy language that can exclude damage when an excluded cause, like flood, combines with a covered cause, like wind. Courts in different states read it differently.
