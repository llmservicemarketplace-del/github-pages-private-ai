Why is my contractor's estimate higher than the insurance estimate?

Leer en español

It's normal for the two numbers to differ. Most of the gap usually comes from a handful of causes, and you can find them by putting the two estimates side by side.

The short answer. Line up both estimates by area and item. Sort every difference into one of the categories below. Then ask the insurer in writing about each item, with the contractor's reasoning and photos.

Where the differences come from

CauseWhat it looks like
ScopeThe contractor includes an area or step the adjuster left out (a second wall, ceiling paint, tear-out).
QuantityDifferent square footage, roof squares or waste factor.
Unit priceLocal labor and material costs differ from the insurer's price list.
Overhead and profitThe contractor charges O&P for coordinating trades; the insurer omitted it. See O&P.
Code itemsThe contractor must meet current code; see ordinance or law.
DepreciationThe insurer's number is actual cash value; the contractor's is full cost. Compare RCV to RCV.
UpgradesThe contractor priced better materials than what was there. These usually aren't owed.

How to line them up

  1. Ask the contractor for an itemized estimate with quantities and units, not a lump sum.
  2. Compare replacement cost totals, not the insurer's ACV payment.
  3. Match items area by area. Mark each one: missing, quantity difference, price difference, or upgrade.
  4. Drop or explain any upgrades; they weaken the rest of the request.
  5. Send the insurer the comparison with photos and ask for a revised estimate or a re-inspection.
  6. If the dispute is only about the amount, appraisal may be available.

A note on contractors and claims

A contractor can explain the work, but many states restrict contractors from negotiating your claim for you unless they hold an adjuster's license. Be careful with any contractor who offers to "handle the insurance" or cover your deductible. See hiring a contractor after a storm and assignment of benefits.

Quick answers

Will insurance pay my contractor's price?

Not automatically. The insurer owes the reasonable cost of the covered repair. A detailed, itemized contractor estimate with photos is the best evidence of what that is.

Should I compare against the check amount?

No. Compare replacement cost to replacement cost. The check is usually actual cash value after depreciation and the deductible.

What if the contractor's estimate includes upgrades?

Remove or separate them. Insurers usually owe like kind and quality, not upgrades.
