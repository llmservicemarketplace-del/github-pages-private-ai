Your denial letter names the reason. Here is what each one means.

Property denial letters look alike: a claim number, a paragraph of policy language, a signature. The sentence that decides what you do next is the one naming the reason. Most fall into three families, with a fourth that is more urgent than the rest.

The short answer. Find the operative sentence, then sort it: a coverage denial says the policy does not apply, a scope denial says the damage is not there or not covered in that amount, a valuation dispute says the number is right, and a procedural letter says you missed a condition. Each needs a different response, and the procedural ones carry deadlines. What to do next is a separate page; this one is about reading what they actually said.

Find the operative sentence

Look for the phrase that names a basis: "not covered," "excluded," "no damage observed," "actual cash value," "conditions precedent," "we are unable to make payment." Everything above it is usually context. Everything below it is usually the appeal-rights boilerplate, which is worth reading once for the deadline and then setting aside.

Coverage denials: the policy is being read against you

These letters argue that the loss falls outside the policy, not that the amount is wrong. Common wording:

  • "not a covered peril," "not sudden and accidental," "excluded under the policy"
  • "wear and tear," "deterioration," "faulty maintenance," "long-term repeated seepage"
  • "no insurable interest," "the policy was not in force on the date of loss"

What to send back: a request for the exact policy section and exclusion the adjuster is relying on, and a written response comparing that language to the facts. Coverage letters are arguments about words, so the words have to be on the table. If the letter quotes an exclusion, check whether an exception to it applies — many policies exclude rot but cover ensuing damage, or exclude repeated seepage but cover a sudden burst.

Scope denials: they say the damage is not there

These are factual disputes dressed as conclusions. Common wording:

  • "no storm-created opening," "no damage observed," "cosmetic only"
  • "pre-existing," "caused by installation," "consistent with maintenance"
  • "the damage is below your deductible"

What to send back: your own dated photographs, a contractor's written scope tied to the observed damage, and a request for a re-inspection with a different adjuster. For roof and structural disputes, an engineer's report is often the item that changes the outcome. The point is not to dispute the adjuster's opinion in the abstract, but to put a competing, documented observation in the file.

Valuation disputes: they say the number is right

The claim is accepted and the payment is short. Common wording:

  • "actual cash value," "depreciation applied," "betterment"
  • "like kind and quality," "we used our pricing software"
  • "overhead and profit is not included," "labor is depreciated"

What to send back: a line-by-line comparison of the carrier's estimate against the repair scope, with the disputed lines named. This is the family where the argument is arithmetical and where a second itemized estimate does most of the work. Some of these disputes are about what the policy allows (labor depreciation, withheld overhead and profit, matching), which is why those topics have their own pages.

Procedural letters: the urgent ones

These do not argue about damage or dollars. They say you did not do something the policy required, and they frequently run alongside a limitation period.

  • "late notice," "no proof of loss was submitted," "failed to cooperate"
  • "misrepresentation," "material change in risk," "vacancy"
  • "we have closed our file"

Treat these as time-critical. A missed proof-of-loss deadline or a limitations period is much harder to fix than a disagreement about a roof. This is the point to talk to a lawyer or a legal-aid clinic rather than to keep corresponding with the adjuster.

What not to do

  • Do not treat the first letter as final. Most policies have a reconsideration path, and several states have a complaint process through the insurance department.
  • Do not sign a release or a settlement agreement to get a partial payment released.
  • Do not send originals. Send copies and keep a dated record of what you sent and when.
  • Do not argue coverage and value in the same letter. They are separate disputes and mixing them lets a carrier answer the easier one.
  • Do not assume the letter's description of your own policy is accurate. Read the policy section it cites.

Where this page stops

This page describes how denial letters are usually written. It is not legal advice, it does not tell you whether a particular denial is right, and it does not replace the policy or the statute in your state. If the letter is procedural, or if a deadline is running, get advice before the deadline rather than after.
