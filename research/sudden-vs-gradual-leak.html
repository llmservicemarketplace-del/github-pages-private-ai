My insurer denied my water claim as a long-term leak. What now?

Leer en español

Most homeowners policies cover sudden and accidental water discharge, such as a burst supply line, but exclude damage from leaks or seepage that continued over weeks or months. Many denials turn on one question: when did the leak start?

The short answer. Read the exact exclusion and any time period it names. Then gather evidence of when the leak began and when you could have known about it. Even when the leak itself isn't covered, some policies still cover the damage it caused, or the cost to reach the leak.

What policies usually say

  • Many forms exclude "continuous or repeated seepage or leakage" over a period of time. Some name a period, such as 14 days or more; others don't.
  • Some exclusions apply only if the leak was hidden or unknown to you; others apply regardless.
  • Wear and tear, rust and deterioration of the plumbing itself are usually excluded, even when the water damage it causes is covered. See wear and tear.
  • Some policies pay to tear out and replace part of the building to reach a covered leak.

Evidence of when it started

  • The plumber's written findings: what failed and how (a burst fitting vs. a pinhole with corrosion).
  • The failed part itself. Keep it and photograph it.
  • Water bills showing a spike, or no spike.
  • Photos showing fresh water vs. long-term staining, rot or mold.
  • When you first noticed it, and what you did that day.

Responding to a denial

  1. Ask for the denial in writing with the exact policy language relied on. See reading a denial letter.
  2. Ask for the evidence behind the conclusion: photos, moisture readings, any expert report.
  3. Send your plumber's findings and your timeline.
  4. Point out any covered parts: resulting damage, tear-out to access, or the time period in the exclusion.
  5. Consider a department of insurance complaint or legal help if the denial seems wrong.

Quick answers

Does homeowners insurance cover slow leaks?

Usually not. Most policies exclude continuous or repeated seepage or leakage over time, though the exact wording and any time limit vary.

How do I prove a leak was sudden?

A plumber's written findings on how the part failed, the failed part itself, water bills, and photos showing fresh water rather than long-term rot or staining.

If the leak isn't covered, is anything covered?

Possibly. Some policies cover resulting damage or the cost to tear out and replace part of the building to reach a covered leak. Read the policy closely.
