Subrogation: When Your Insurer Goes After the Party at Fault

Last reviewed November 10, 2026. Free public guidance from The New Faith Church and Co-op, Inc. (NFCcop), a 501(c)(3) nonprofit. This is general information, not legal or professional advice. Rules differ by state and change over time, so confirm with the official sources below.

If someone else caused your damage (a neighbor's contractor, a faulty appliance, another driver), your insurer can pay your claim and then try to recover the money from the responsible party. That's called subrogation. If it succeeds, you may get some or all of your deductible back.

How subrogation usually works

  1. You file with your own insurer and pay your deductible.
  2. Your insurer pays the covered loss.
  3. Your insurer pursues the at-fault party or their insurer to recover what it paid.
  4. If money is recovered, your deductible is often refunded in full or in part.

Getting your deductible back

  • Ask your adjuster whether subrogation is being pursued and ask to be told the outcome.
  • Whether you get all or part of your deductible depends on your policy, your state's law, and how much is recovered. Many insurers split a partial recovery proportionally.
  • Some states follow a made-whole rule, meaning you must be fully paid before the insurer keeps any recovery, but states differ and policies can change how it applies.
  • Subrogation can take months or years. Keep your claim number and check in.

Common subrogation situations

What happenedWho may be responsible
A water heater, washer hose, or appliance failedThe manufacturer or installer
A contractor caused a fire or leakThe contractor or their insurer
Another driver hit your carThe other driver's insurer
A neighbor's tree fell on your houseDepends. See tree fell on house: who pays

Mistakes to avoid

  • Don't sign a release or accept a settlement from the at-fault party without telling your insurer. It can block subrogation and affect your claim.
  • Keep the evidence: the failed part, hose, or appliance. Don't throw it away until your insurer says it's okay.
  • Cooperate with reasonable requests for statements and documents. Your policy likely requires it.

Frequently asked questions

What does subrogation mean in insurance?

It's when your insurer, after paying your claim, seeks repayment from the person or company that caused the loss.

Will I get my deductible back?

Often, if the insurer recovers money. You may get all or part of it, depending on your policy, state law, and how much is recovered.

Can I settle with the at-fault party myself?

Tell your insurer first. Signing a release without their consent can hurt their recovery and your coverage.
