How long do I have to sue my insurance company?

Most homeowners policies have a "suit against us" clause: you can't sue unless you've met the policy conditions, and you must sue within a set time, often one or two years. State law can override a policy that sets a shorter period than allowed.

Missing the deadline can end your claim permanently, even if the insurer was wrong.

The short answer. Find the "suit against us" or "legal action" clause in your policy today and write the deadline on your calendar. Then check your state's law, which may give you more time or add pre-suit notice requirements. Talk to an attorney well before the deadline. Rules differ by state and by policy wording.

What this turns on

  • Your policy's suit limitation clause.
  • Your state's minimum limitation period and when it starts.
  • Pre-suit notice requirements in your state.

State examples

  • Texas: a contract can't set a limitation period shorter than two years. The period runs from when the claim accrues, often when the insurer denies or underpays.
  • Florida: notice of a new or reopened claim must be given within one year of the date of loss, and supplemental claims within 18 months. Florida also changed its property insurance lawsuit rules in 2022 and 2023, including pre-suit notice requirements. Sources describe the current lawsuit deadline differently, so confirm it with an attorney or the current text of section 95.11 of the Florida Statutes.
Filing a complaint, appraisal, or mediation may not pause the deadline to sue. Don't assume it does.

What your file needs

  • Your full policy, including the legal action clause.
  • Date of loss and date of denial or underpayment.
  • Your claim log.

Quick answers

How long do I have to sue my homeowners insurance company?

It depends on your policy and state law. Many policies say one or two years. Some states set minimums; Texas, for example, doesn't allow a contractual period shorter than two years.

Does appraisal or a complaint stop the deadline to sue?

Not necessarily. Don't assume it does. Check with an attorney.
