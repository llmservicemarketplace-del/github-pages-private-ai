What should I look for before I sign my insurer's release?

Leer en español

A release is a contract. Once signed, it usually ends your right to more money for whatever it covers. Many problems come from releases that are broader than the deal you thought you made.

The short answer. Read every line. Make sure the release matches what was agreed: the amount, this claim, this loss, these parties, and nothing more. Ask for changes in writing before you sign. If the amount is significant, have a lawyer review it.

Checklist

  1. Amount and payees. The figure matches the agreement; the check will name the right payees, including any mortgage company. See mortgage company on the check.
  2. Who is released. Only the insurer? Or also its adjusters, contractors, agents, affiliates, "and all other persons"? A broad list can release people you may have claims against.
  3. What is released. Only this claim, this policy and this date of loss? Watch for "any and all claims," "known and unknown," and "arising out of or related to."
  4. Open items. Recoverable depreciation, pending supplements, contents, ALE, and code upgrades: are they included, excluded or preserved?
  5. Bad-faith and statutory claims. Does it waive claims for bad faith, interest, penalties or attorney's fees?
  6. Lawsuits. If a case is pending, does it require dismissal "with prejudice," and who pays costs and fees?
  7. Confidentiality and non-disparagement. Are you agreeing to keep the settlement secret or not to criticize the insurer?
  8. Who signs. Every named insured may need to sign. If only one person agreed to the deal, the others aren't bound by that agreement.
  9. Payment timing. When the money will be paid after you sign.

Checks marked "full and final"

In many states, cashing a check that clearly says it's in full satisfaction of a disputed claim can settle the claim, even without a signed release. This is a legal doctrine called "accord and satisfaction." If a check carries that kind of wording and you don't agree it's full payment, get advice before depositing it.

Asking for changes

You can propose edits: limit it to "this claim, Claim No. ___, for the [date] loss, under Policy No. ___," name only the insurer, and carve out recoverable depreciation or pending supplements. Send your edits in writing and keep the exchange.

Quick answers

Do I have to sign the release my insurer sent?

No. A release is part of a settlement agreement. You can propose changes so it matches the deal you made, or decline to sign.

Can a release waive claims I don't know about yet?

Often yes, if it releases 'known and unknown' claims. Limit it to the specific claim and loss if that's what you agreed to.

Is it risky to cash a check marked 'full and final payment'?

It can be. In many states, cashing a check clearly offered in full satisfaction of a disputed claim can settle it. Get advice before depositing one you disagree with.
