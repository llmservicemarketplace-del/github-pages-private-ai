Does Florida require the insurer to match my roof shingles?

When a storm takes part of a roof, the replacement shingles usually do not match the discontinued original. Florida is one of the states that addresses this in statute rather than leaving it entirely to the policy wording. The statute is real, and it is narrower than "the whole roof has to be replaced."

The short answer. Under Fla. Stat. § 626.9744(2), when replaced items do not match in quality, color or size, the insurer must make reasonable repairs or replacement of items in adjoining areas — and the statute lists the factors that decide how far that goes. It is not an automatic entitlement to a full roof.

What the statute says

Section 626.9744 opens by limiting itself to homeowner policies that settle first-party losses on a repair or replacement cost basis, "unless otherwise provided by the policy." Subsection (2) then reads:

When a loss requires replacement of items and the replaced items do not match in quality, color, or size, the insurer shall make reasonable repairs or replacement of items in adjoining areas. In determining the extent of the repairs or replacement of items in adjoining areas, the insurer may consider the cost of repairing or replacing the undamaged portions of the property, the degree of uniformity that can be achieved without such cost, the remaining useful life of the undamaged portion, and other relevant factors.

Two neighbours of that subsection matter in practice. Subsection (1) requires physical damage incurred in making a covered repair to be included in the loss, and stops the insured from being charged for code-required betterment beyond the deductible unless the policy excludes or limits it. Subsection (3) says the insurer is not a warrantor of the repairs.

What it does not settle

  • "Adjoining areas" is not defined. One slope, one elevation and one continuous run are different arguments, and the statute does not pick one for you.
  • "Reasonable" cuts both ways. The four listed factors — cost of the undamaged portions, achievable uniformity without that cost, remaining useful life, other relevant factors — give the insurer discretion the statute does not remove.
  • The policy can change the answer. The section applies "unless otherwise provided by the policy," so the form has to be read before the statute.
  • It does not set a deadline and it does not decide actual cash value versus replacement cost; those come from other parts of the policy and the claims statutes.

How to use it on a claim

  1. Prove the mismatch. Photograph the continuous area, not just the damaged slope. Record the product name, manufacturer, and any label or stamp on the back of a shingle.
  2. Prove availability. A written supplier response that the original is discontinued does more than an assertion that it "probably" is.
  3. Get the boundary explained. Ask the adjuster, in writing, how the proposed repair boundary applies the four factors in subsection (2).
  4. Keep it factual. Separate appearance arguments from installation requirements: a sound partial repair is a different question from a repair that cannot be made to look uniform.
  5. Escalate on paper. If the reasoning never arrives, a written complaint to the Florida Department of Financial Services creates a record.
