Can I sue my insurance company without a lawyer?

Individuals can generally represent themselves in court, including in a lawsuit against their own insurer. A business entity usually can't; it needs a lawyer. Suing is a serious step with strict rules and deadlines, and the insurer will have experienced counsel.

The short answer. Before filing, check your lawsuit deadline, complete any required pre-suit notice or demand, and organize your evidence. Talk to a lawyer or legal aid first if you can; many insurance lawyers offer free consultations.

Before you file

  1. Check the deadline. Your policy and state law limit how long you have to sue. See lawsuit deadlines.
  2. Use the remedies short of suit. Appraisal, mediation and a department of insurance complaint can resolve some disputes. See appraisal and mediation.
  3. Complete pre-suit steps.
    • Florida: before suing under a residential or commercial property policy, a claimant must give written notice of intent to initiate litigation through the Department of Financial Services at least 10 business days before filing, on the department's form. After a denial it includes an estimate of damages, if known; otherwise it includes a pre-suit settlement demand itemizing damages and the disputed amount. The insurer must respond in writing within 10 business days (Fla. Stat. 627.70152).
    • Georgia: to seek the bad-faith penalty, send a demand and allow 60 days before suing. See Georgia's 60-day demand.
    • Florida bad faith: a civil remedy notice has its own rules. See Florida civil remedy notice.
  4. Organize the record: policy, estimates, photos, correspondence and your claim log, by date.

The lawsuit, step by step

  1. Complaint. Usually breach of contract: you had a policy, suffered a covered loss, complied with the policy's conditions, and the insurer didn't pay what's owed. Some states add statutory bad-faith claims with their own requirements.
  2. Filing and fees. File in the right court for the amount and county. Ask about a fee waiver if needed.
  3. Service. Serve the insurer properly, often through its registered agent or your state's insurance commissioner. See serving court papers. Make sure proof of service is filed.
  4. The insurer's answer. Expect an answer with defenses, or a motion to dismiss. Insurers often argue late notice, failure to comply with policy conditions, exclusions, or that a settlement was already reached. See is a settlement offer binding?
  5. Discovery, mediation, trial. See discovery requests and preparing for mediation.

When to get a lawyer

  • Large losses, or claims of fraud or misrepresentation against you.
  • Bad-faith or extra-contractual claims.
  • Any motion you don't understand, or a deadline you're unsure of.

Quick answers

Can I sue my homeowners insurance company myself?

Individuals can generally represent themselves. Courts expect self-represented parties to follow the same rules and deadlines, and a business entity usually needs a lawyer.

Do I have to notify my insurer before suing in Florida?

Yes. Florida requires written notice of intent to initiate litigation through the Department of Financial Services at least 10 business days before filing suit under a property policy.

What will the insurer argue?

Common defenses include late notice, failure to comply with policy conditions, exclusions, the amount of loss, and that the claim was already settled.
