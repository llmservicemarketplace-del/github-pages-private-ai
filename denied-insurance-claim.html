How to Appeal a Denied Home Insurance Claim

Your claim was denied. Or the repair offer will not cover the work. Start with the insurer's written reasons, identify the evidence that answers them, and prepare a documented response.

This free guide explains how to dispute a property damage estimate or request reconsideration of a denial. You can use every step without buying software.

Denied coverage or a low settlement? Identify the dispute first.

The insurer says the loss is not covered

An appeal needs to address the stated reason: an exclusion, the cause of damage, a policy condition, or missing documentation. A higher repair estimate alone may not answer a coverage denial.

The insurer accepts damage but offers too little

Compare the scope, measurements, repair methods, unit prices, deductible, and depreciation. Identify each disputed item and the record supporting a correction or supplement.

Check deadlines before doing anything else. Review notice, proof-of-loss, replacement-cost, appeal, and lawsuit requirements in your policy and denial letter. Do not assume a reconsideration request or regulatory complaint pauses any deadline. Seek qualified help promptly if time is short.

What might be missing from the first property damage estimate?

Review these potential gaps rather than assuming every item is owed. Payment depends on your policy, the repair facts, and applicable state law.

  • Overhead & Profit (O&P): Does the estimate account for general-contractor coordination where the repair reasonably requires it? Document the work and coordination needed; there is no universal automatic percentage.
  • Code upgrades / Ordinance or Law: Ask the building department or contractor to identify required upgrades. Check your endorsement, limits, exclusions, and any requirement to incur the expense.
  • Matching rules: Can replacement roofing, siding, or flooring reasonably match what remains? Document material availability and appearance. State rules and policy terms differ; a mismatch does not automatically require full replacement.
  • Labor depreciation: Ask whether depreciation was applied to labor as well as materials, and request the calculation and policy basis. Whether labor can be depreciated depends on applicable law and policy language.

Also distinguish actual cash value (ACV) from replacement cost value (RCV). Some replacement-cost payments require completed repairs and supporting records within specified deadlines. Read Tennessee's ACV/RCV explainer; your state's rules may differ.

How to formally dispute a denial: four steps, in order

Use your insurer's required procedure and submission channel. This is a general administrative blueprint; there is no single nationwide homeowners insurance appeal form or deadline.

  1. Get the written decision and complete policy

    Request the denial or partial-denial explanation, cited provisions, itemized estimate, and relevant inspection findings. Gather the declarations, policy form, and endorsements in effect on the loss date. Ask where to send a reconsideration request and what documents and deadlines apply.

  2. Build an evidence file that answers the decision

    Keep original photos, dates, measurements, receipts, contractor estimates, and correspondence. For a denial, connect each response to evidence about the stated reason. For underpayment, make a comparison with four columns: disputed item, insurer's allowance, requested correction, and supporting document. Obtain qualified causation or repair opinions when needed.

  3. Submit a written reconsideration request or supplement

    Include your claim number, loss date, disputed decision, requested correction, and a numbered attachment list. Explain each point briefly and identify its evidence. Submit copies through the required channel, keep the originals and delivery confirmation, and request acknowledgment and a written response. Complete any separately required sworn proof of loss.

  4. Follow up and choose the appropriate escalation

    Ask for claims-supervisor review if the response does not address your evidence. Contact your state insurance department about unresolved handling concerns. Discuss appraisal for eligible valuation disputes, available mediation, or legal options with a qualified professional. Appraisal generally addresses value rather than all coverage questions; a regulator's complaint process does not replace a lawsuit.

The NAIC settlement guide explains written denials and supporting records. The Texas Department of Insurance dispute guide describes escalation options; availability and rules vary by state. NFIP flood claims have a separate FEMA appeal process; follow the instructions in the flood denial letter.
