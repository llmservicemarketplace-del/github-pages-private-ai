Federal Government Surplus and Seized Property Auctions

Last reviewed November 14, 2026. Written by The New Faith Church and Co-op, Inc. (NFCcop), a 501(c)(3) nonprofit educational resource. General information only, not legal advice. We are not a government agency and we charge nothing for these guides.

Short answer: Most federal surplus is sold online at GSA Auctions, where you can filter by state. Seized and forfeited property is sold through the Treasury Department and the U.S. Marshals Service. All of the listings are free to view.

Federal agencies sell what they no longer need and what the government has seized. This page lists the official places to look, how bidding works, and what to watch for. For houses and land, see federal real estate for sale.

Do not pay for a list. Government sale lists are free on the government's own website. Sites that charge a fee or a subscription for "government foreclosure lists" or "secret auction access" are selling public information. Start from a .gov address or the office's own page, and follow its link to the auction.

How federal auctions work

  1. Register on the auction site. Registration is free.
  2. Read the lot description and terms. They say where the item is, when you can inspect it, how to pay, and how long you have to remove it.
  3. Inspect if you can. Items are sold as-is, and descriptions are not guarantees.
  4. Bid. Sales may be online, in person, or by mail-in bid. Some are run by the agency and some by an auction company under contract.
  5. Pay and pick up on time. You arrange and pay for removal. Missing the deadline can cost you the item and your right to bid again.

What to check before you bid

  • Sold as-is. There is usually no inspection, no warranty, and no refund. For real estate you often cannot go inside before you bid.
  • Title is your job. Some liens, easements, and other claims can survive a sale. A title search before bidding is the only way to know. Title insurance can be hard to get on tax-sale property without more legal work.
  • People may live there. If the property is occupied, removing anyone takes a court process that you pay for.
  • Payment is fast. Many sales want a deposit that day and the balance within days, by certified funds.
  • Costs keep coming. Later taxes, code violations, demolition orders, and cleanup can follow the property to the buyer.
  • Buyer's fees. Online auctions often add a buyer's premium on top of the bid.

Surplus that is not sold to the public

Before federal surplus reaches public auction, it is first offered to other federal agencies, then to state and local governments and eligible nonprofits through each state's surplus agency. If you run a nonprofit, see surplus property for nonprofits.

State and local sales

States, counties, and cities run their own sales. Choose your state: government auctions by state.

Common questions

Where does the federal government sell surplus property?

Mainly on GSA Auctions, which lists federal surplus vehicles, equipment, furniture, and electronics by state. The Treasury Department and the U.S. Marshals Service sell seized and forfeited property through their own programs.

Can anyone bid at a federal surplus auction?

Generally yes. Most federal auctions are open to the public once you register on the auction site. Each sale has its own terms on payment, pickup, and who may bid.

Are government auction lists free?

Yes. Federal agencies post their sales for free. You do not need to buy a list or a membership.
