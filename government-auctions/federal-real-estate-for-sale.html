Federal Real Estate for Sale: Homes, Buildings, and Land

Last reviewed November 14, 2026. Written by The New Faith Church and Co-op, Inc. (NFCcop), a 501(c)(3) nonprofit educational resource. General information only, not legal advice. We are not a government agency and we charge nothing for these guides.

Short answer: Federal agencies sell real estate on their own sites: HUD for FHA foreclosures, USDA for rural homes and farms, Fannie Mae for its foreclosed homes, the FDIC for failed-bank property, and GSA for surplus federal buildings and land. Each can be searched by state, for free.

The federal government ends up owning real estate through loan foreclosures, bank failures, forfeiture, and buildings it no longer needs. This page lists the official places to look and how each one sells.

Do not pay for a list. Government sale lists are free on the government's own website. Sites that charge a fee or a subscription for "government foreclosure lists" or "secret auction access" are selling public information. Start from a .gov address or the office's own page, and follow its link to the auction.

Seized real estate is also sold through U.S. Treasury auctions and the U.S. Marshals Service. The government's own overview is at USA.gov: real estate and federal lands for sale.

How these sales differ

  • HUD homes are sold by bid through a real estate agent registered with HUD. Buyers who will live in the home usually get a first bidding period before investors.
  • USDA properties are sold by auction or offer, depending on the property.
  • Fannie Mae and FDIC properties are listed with local real estate agents like other homes.
  • GSA real estate is sold by online auction, with a registration deposit and set bidding dates.
  • Federal land from the Bureau of Land Management is sold only when a specific parcel is identified for sale, by competitive bid at no less than appraised value.

Before you make an offer

  • Sold as-is. There is usually no inspection, no warranty, and no refund. For real estate you often cannot go inside before you bid.
  • Title is your job. Some liens, easements, and other claims can survive a sale. A title search before bidding is the only way to know. Title insurance can be hard to get on tax-sale property without more legal work.
  • People may live there. If the property is occupied, removing anyone takes a court process that you pay for.
  • Payment is fast. Many sales want a deposit that day and the balance within days, by certified funds.
  • Costs keep coming. Later taxes, code violations, demolition orders, and cleanup can follow the property to the buyer.
  • Buyer's fees. Online auctions often add a buyer's premium on top of the bid.

You can use a mortgage on many of these homes, but a house in poor condition may not qualify for a standard loan. Ask a lender early. Also see researching foreclosed and bank-owned property, hazard and flood research, and the foreclosure scam guide.

State and local real estate sales

Tax sales, sheriff's sales, and land bank sales are local. Choose your state: government auctions by state. For how tax sales work, see tax lien and tax deed sales explained.

Common questions

Where can I find government-owned homes for sale?

HUD lists homes on the HUD Home Store, USDA lists rural homes and farms on its resales site, Fannie Mae lists homes on HomePath, and the FDIC lists real estate from failed banks. GSA auctions surplus federal buildings and land.

Are government homes cheaper?

Not automatically. They are priced or auctioned near market value and sold as-is. The savings, if any, come from condition and from buyers who can handle repairs.

Can I buy federal land?

Sometimes. The Bureau of Land Management occasionally sells parcels of undeveloped public land, mostly in western states, and GSA sells surplus federal real estate. There is no program that gives away free federal land.
