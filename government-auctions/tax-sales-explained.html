Tax Lien and Tax Deed Sales Explained, State by State

Last reviewed November 14, 2026. Written by The New Faith Church and Co-op, Inc. (NFCcop), a 501(c)(3) nonprofit educational resource. General information only, not legal advice. We are not a government agency and we charge nothing for these guides.

Short answer: When property taxes go unpaid, the government sells either the debt (a tax lien) or the property (a tax deed). Some states sell the property but let the former owner buy it back for a time. Which one you are bidding on changes everything about the risk.

Tax sale lists are public and free, and they attract a lot of expensive courses and paid lists. This page explains the three systems in plain language, who runs the sale in each state, and what can go wrong.

Do not pay for a list. Government sale lists are free on the government's own website. Sites that charge a fee or a subscription for "government foreclosure lists" or "secret auction access" are selling public information. Start from a .gov address or the office's own page, and follow its link to the auction.

The three systems

Tax lien sales

The government sells its claim for the unpaid taxes. The buyer pays the taxes and holds a lien. If the owner pays, the buyer gets the money back with interest or a penalty set by state law. If the owner never pays, the buyer can start the legal steps to take the property, which take time and must be done exactly right.

Tax deed sales

The government forecloses first, then sells the property at auction. The buyer gets a deed. The deed is often not a clean title: many buyers need a court case to clear the title before they can get title insurance, borrow against the property, or resell it.

Redeemable sales

The buyer gets a deed or a certificate for the property, but the former owner can redeem for a period after the sale by paying the price plus a penalty. Until that period ends, do not spend money improving the property.

What can go wrong

  • Sold as-is. There is usually no inspection, no warranty, and no refund. For real estate you often cannot go inside before you bid.
  • Title is your job. Some liens, easements, and other claims can survive a sale. A title search before bidding is the only way to know. Title insurance can be hard to get on tax-sale property without more legal work.
  • People may live there. If the property is occupied, removing anyone takes a court process that you pay for.
  • Payment is fast. Many sales want a deposit that day and the balance within days, by certified funds.
  • Costs keep coming. Later taxes, code violations, demolition orders, and cleanup can follow the property to the buyer.
  • Buyer's fees. Online auctions often add a buyer's premium on top of the bid.
  • Worthless parcels. Tax sale lists include drainage ditches, strips with no access, and lots that cannot be built on.
  • Bankruptcy and notice problems. A sale can be undone if the owner was in bankruptcy or was not given proper notice.
  • Federal liens. An IRS lien can give the federal government its own right to redeem after a sale.

If you are the owner

You usually have the right to pay before the sale and, in many states, to redeem after it. If the sale brings more than you owed, you may be able to claim the surplus. Do not sign anything from a company offering to "recover" money for a share until you have asked the tax office how to claim it yourself. Common questions

What is the difference between a tax lien and a tax deed?

A tax lien sale sells the debt: the buyer pays the back taxes and is repaid with interest if the owner redeems. A tax deed sale sells the property itself after the government forecloses. A redeemable sale sells the property but gives the former owner time to buy it back.

Do I get the house if I buy a tax lien?

Usually not. Most liens are paid off by the owner. Getting the property takes more time, more notices, and often a court case, and the rules are strict.

Does a tax sale wipe out the mortgage?

It depends on the state and the type of sale. Some claims are cut off and others survive. Check with a title company or a real estate lawyer before you bid, not after.

What happens to extra money from a tax sale?

If a property sells for more than the taxes and costs owed, the former owner may have a claim to the surplus. Each state has its own process and deadline for claiming it.
