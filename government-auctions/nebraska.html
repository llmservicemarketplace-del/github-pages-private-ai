Nebraska Government Auctions: Surplus Property, Tax Sales, and Real Estate

Last reviewed November 14, 2026. Written by The New Faith Church and Co-op, Inc. (NFCcop), a 501(c)(3) nonprofit educational resource. General information only, not legal advice. We are not a government agency and we charge nothing for these guides.

Short answer: In Nebraska there are four places to look. State surplus goes through Nebraska Federal Surplus Property (402-471-2677). Tax-delinquent real estate is sold by the county treasurer. Foreclosure and sheriff's sales are run county by county. Federal property in Nebraska is listed on federal sites you can search by state.

Every official list is free. You should never have to pay to see what is for sale.

Governments sell things they no longer need and property they have taken for unpaid taxes or through court cases. That includes vehicles, equipment, office furniture, vacant lots, houses, and land. This page maps who sells what in Nebraska at the state, local, and federal level, and what to check before you bid.

State surplus property in Nebraska

The federal government lists Nebraska Federal Surplus Property as Nebraska's state agency for surplus property. Phone: 402-471-2677.

How Nebraska sells to the public: Nebraska's Materiel Division Surplus Property office processes and sells state surplus. Start at Nebraska state surplus property (official site).

The office listed by GSA runs the federal surplus program for local governments and eligible nonprofits. In most states the same office, or one beside it, also sells the state's own surplus to the public: retired vehicles, heavy equipment, computers, furniture, and tools. Ask three questions when you call:

  • How does the public buy state surplus: a warehouse store, online auctions, or live auctions?
  • Which website carries the listings, and do I need to register first?
  • Does the state sell surplus real estate, and which agency handles it?

State land and buildings are usually sold by a different agency than equipment. The transportation department often sells leftover right-of-way parcels, and a land office or general services agency may sell other state real estate.

The state treasurer or comptroller also auctions unclaimed property from abandoned safe deposit boxes from time to time. Find Nebraska's program through unclaimed.org, and check it for money owed to you while you are there.

Tax-delinquent property in Nebraska

Nebraska sells tax liens. The winning bidder pays the back taxes and gets a lien on the property, not the property itself. The owner can pay the bidder back with interest. Only if the owner does not, and the bidder completes more legal steps, can the bidder end up with the property.

Who runs it: the county treasurer. That office publishes the sale date, the list of parcels, bidder registration rules, and how to pay. Dates, redemption periods, and interest rates are set by state law and change, so take them from the office's current notice and not from an investing website.

Before you bid on any parcel:

  1. Read the official notice and terms for that sale, start to finish.
  2. Look up the parcel. Use the assessor's and recorder's records to see the owner, the legal description, and what is recorded against it. See the Nebraska public records guide.
  3. Go look at it from the street. Vacant lots, landlocked strips, and condemned buildings are common on tax sale lists.
  4. Find out what survives the sale. Ask a title company or a real estate lawyer which liens and claims stay with the property.
  5. Know the owner's rights. Learn whether the former owner can redeem after the sale, and for how long.

For how the three systems differ, see tax lien and tax deed sales explained.

Foreclosure and sheriff's sales

These are separate from tax sales. A lender forecloses on a mortgage, or a creditor collects a court judgment, and the property is sold at public auction. Depending on the case, the sale is held by the county sheriff, a court officer, or a trustee named in the loan papers.

  • Sales are announced as legal notices and usually posted on the sheriff's or court's website.
  • The court file shows what is owed and who has a claim. See find a courthouse in Nebraska.
  • After a foreclosure, the bank or federal agency that ends up with the house sells it through regular listings. See researching foreclosed and bank-owned property.

City, county, and other local sales

Local governments in Nebraska sell their own surplus. There is no single statewide list, so check each one:

  • Cities and county governments: retired police cars, trucks, mowers, and office equipment, through the purchasing, fleet, or finance office.
  • Police and sheriff's departments: unclaimed, found, and forfeited property.
  • School districts, colleges, and utilities: buses, computers, kitchen and shop equipment.
  • Land banks and city-owned lots: many cities sell vacant lots and houses they hold, sometimes to neighbors or to buyers who agree to repair them.
  • Housing and redevelopment agencies: lots and buildings offered for redevelopment.

To find them, go to the government's own website and search for "surplus," "auction," or "purchasing." Many use an outside online auction company to run the bidding. That is normal, but reach it through the link on the government's own site so you know the sale is real. Find local government sites through USA.gov's local government directory. What to check before you bid on anything

  • Sold as-is. There is usually no inspection, no warranty, and no refund. For real estate you often cannot go inside before you bid.
  • Title is your job. Some liens, easements, and other claims can survive a sale. A title search before bidding is the only way to know. Title insurance can be hard to get on tax-sale property without more legal work.
  • People may live there. If the property is occupied, removing anyone takes a court process that you pay for.
  • Payment is fast. Many sales want a deposit that day and the balance within days, by certified funds.
  • Costs keep coming. Later taxes, code violations, demolition orders, and cleanup can follow the property to the buyer.
  • Buyer's fees. Online auctions often add a buyer's premium on top of the bid.

If you run a nonprofit or work for a local government

You may not need to bid at all. Eligible nonprofits and public agencies can get federal surplus through Nebraska Federal Surplus Property for a service charge instead of the market price. See surplus property for nonprofits.

If it is your property being sold

If your home is headed to a tax sale or foreclosure, you may still be able to pay, set up a payment plan, or redeem after the sale. If a tax sale brings more than you owed, you may have a claim to the extra money. Act early and get free advice: free legal aid in Nebraska and mortgage forbearance.

Common questions

Where can I buy government surplus in Nebraska?

Nebraska's Materiel Division Surplus Property office processes and sells state surplus. The state surplus office is Nebraska Federal Surplus Property, 402-471-2677. Also check your city and county websites for their own surplus auctions, and search GSA Auctions by state for federal surplus.

Is Nebraska a tax lien state or a tax deed state?

Nebraska sells tax liens. The winning bidder pays the back taxes and gets a lien on the property, not the property itself. The owner can pay the bidder back with interest. Only if the owner does not, and the bidder completes more legal steps, can the bidder end up with the property.

Who runs tax sales in Nebraska?

Tax sales are run by the county treasurer. Sale dates, bidder registration, and the list of properties come from that office, and the list is free.

How do I find foreclosure and sheriff's sales in Nebraska?

Mortgage foreclosure and judgment sales are announced by the sheriff, the court, or a trustee, and published as legal notices. Check the county sheriff's website and the court clerk for the county where the property sits.
