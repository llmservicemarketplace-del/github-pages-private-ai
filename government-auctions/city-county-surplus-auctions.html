City and County Surplus Auctions and Local Government Sales

Last reviewed November 14, 2026. Written by The New Faith Church and Co-op, Inc. (NFCcop), a 501(c)(3) nonprofit educational resource. General information only, not legal advice. We are not a government agency and we charge nothing for these guides.

Short answer: Cities, counties, school districts, and police departments sell their own surplus, and each one posts its own sales. Start at the government's website, search for "surplus" or "auction," and follow its link to the bidding site.

Local governments replace vehicles and equipment on a schedule and sell the old ones to the public. They also sell property that came to them through unpaid taxes, forfeiture, or abandonment. There is no national list, so this page explains who sells what and how to find it.

Do not pay for a list. Government sale lists are free on the government's own website. Sites that charge a fee or a subscription for "government foreclosure lists" or "secret auction access" are selling public information. Start from a .gov address or the office's own page, and follow its link to the auction.

How to find local sales

  1. Find the official website. Use USA.gov's local government directory if you are not sure of the address.
  2. Search the site for "surplus," "auction," "purchasing," "land bank," or "tax sale."
  3. Follow the government's own link to the auction. Many use an outside online auction company. That is normal.
  4. Read the legal notices. Sales of real estate are advertised in the local newspaper of record and often on the clerk's or sheriff's page.
  5. Call the office and ask when the next sale is and how to get on the notice list. It is free.

Buying city-owned lots and land bank property

Land banks and city real estate offices often care more about what happens to the property than about the price. Expect an application instead of an open auction, a plan and budget for repairs, proof you are current on your own taxes, and deadlines to finish the work. Neighbors can sometimes buy an adjoining vacant lot at low cost.

Before you bid

  • Sold as-is. There is usually no inspection, no warranty, and no refund. For real estate you often cannot go inside before you bid.
  • Title is your job. Some liens, easements, and other claims can survive a sale. A title search before bidding is the only way to know. Title insurance can be hard to get on tax-sale property without more legal work.
  • People may live there. If the property is occupied, removing anyone takes a court process that you pay for.
  • Payment is fast. Many sales want a deposit that day and the balance within days, by certified funds.
  • Costs keep coming. Later taxes, code violations, demolition orders, and cleanup can follow the property to the buyer.
  • Buyer's fees. Online auctions often add a buyer's premium on top of the bid.

Common questions

How do I find city or county surplus auctions near me?

Go to the city or county's own website and search for surplus, auction, or purchasing. The purchasing, fleet, or finance office posts sales, often with a link to an online auction company that runs the bidding.

What do cities and counties sell?

Retired police cars and trucks, mowers and heavy equipment, office furniture, computers, and unclaimed or forfeited property held by police. Many also sell vacant lots and houses through a land bank or real estate office.

Are online government auction sites legitimate?

Many governments do use outside auction companies. To be sure a sale is real, reach it through the link on the government's own website instead of through an ad or a search result.
