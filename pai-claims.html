The problem with a claim spread across twelve folders

A property damage claim generates paper faster than almost anything else in ordinary life. Within a few weeks of a storm, a fire, or a burst supply line, a homeowner typically has several hundred photographs on a phone, a carrier estimate running to twenty or thirty pages of line items, one or two contractor bids, a policy declarations page, an adjuster's business card, a scope sheet, and an email thread that has already forked into four sub-threads.

Most people keep this in whatever is closest to hand: photos stay on the phone, the estimate sits in a Downloads folder, the emails stay in the inbox, and the contractor's quote is a paper sheet on the kitchen table. Each item is fine on its own. What goes missing is the connection between them — which photograph supports which line item, which version of the estimate the carrier actually paid on, and what was said on the call three weeks ago that changed the scope.

That connection is exactly what matters if the claim is underpaid, denied, or reopened as a supplement. A photograph with no date, no location, and no link to a repair line is much weaker evidence than the same photograph tied to the item it documents.

PAi Claims exists to hold that structure in one place, on hardware you own.

Why carrier values are kept separate from yours

An insurance estimate is not a neutral document. It is one party's opinion of scope, quantity, and unit price, produced with software the carrier licenses and pricing data the carrier subscribes to. It may be entirely reasonable. It may also miss a room, price a material at a grade below what was there, or omit the labor steps between demolition and finish.

The only way to know is to compare it line by line against an independently built quantity takeoff. That comparison falls apart if the two sets of numbers get mixed together in a single spreadsheet, which is what usually happens when someone tries to do this in Excel. Once carrier quantities and your own quantities occupy the same cells, the original position is gone and the disagreement becomes impossible to articulate.

PAi Claims keeps the two sets structurally distinct. The carrier's numbers stay as received. Your own preliminary quantities and prices sit alongside, each traceable back to the measurement or quote it came from. The delta between them is a result, not a guess.

Provenance, and why it matters more than a total

Any tool can produce a number. The useful question is where the number came from. A figure derived from a measured wall area, a figure copied from a contractor's bid, and a figure a language model produced from a photograph are three different kinds of claim, and treating them identically is how a well-intentioned file becomes indefensible.

PAi Claims marks the difference. Supported facts — things with a document, measurement, or record behind them — are held apart from assumptions and from AI-generated inferences. If a figure has to be defended later, the basis for it is attached to it rather than remembered.

This matters most in the situation nobody plans for: the claim that goes from routine to contested. At that point the file stops being a personal organizing aid and starts being a record someone else will read critically.

Everything stays on your computer

PAi Claims is a PAi product, which means it loads its AI model onto your machine during setup and runs it there. After installation the software works with no network connection, no account, and no data leaving the computer. There is no server holding your claim file, because there is no server.

This is a deliberate design choice rather than a marketing position. Claim files contain photographs of the inside of your home, your policy number, your address, records of what you were paid and when, and correspondence you may not want retained by a third party indefinitely. The safest place for that material is a machine you physically control.

If you want a larger model than a desktop can hold, that path exists through the PSi line, and it is off until you enable it and supply your own provider credentials.

Common questions

Does PAi Claims send my claim files to a server?

No. The case folder lives on your computer. There is no account to create and no upload step, and the software runs with the network disconnected.

Do I need AI to use it?

No. Organizing evidence, comparing estimates, and building repair quantities all work without AI. A compatible local model can be added on your own hardware, and any hosted model requires your own credentials and explicit approval.

Will it tell me what my claim is worth?

No, and treat anything that claims otherwise with suspicion. It organizes the evidence and makes the gap between positions visible. What that gap is worth depends on your policy language, your jurisdiction, and facts the software cannot see.

Is this a replacement for a public adjuster or an attorney?

No. It is an organizational and analytical aid. It does not interpret your policy, give legal advice, or represent you with a carrier.

What does it run on?

Windows 10 and Windows 11, 64-bit. It installs from the Microsoft Store.

AI is optional

Core estimating and comparison tools work without AI. Compatible local AI can run on the user's computer. Hosted AI requires explicit approval and the user's own provider credentials.

PAi Claims is an organizational and analytical aid. It is not an insurer, public adjuster, law firm, or substitute for licensed professional advice. Verify policy interpretations, deadlines, building-code requirements, quantities, prices, and generated documents.
