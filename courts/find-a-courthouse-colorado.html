Find a Courthouse in Colorado: Official Court and Clerk Directories

Last reviewed November 12, 2026. Written by The New Faith Church and Co-op, Inc. (NFCcop), a 501(c)(3) nonprofit educational resource. General information only, not legal advice. We sell no legal, search, background-check, or filing services.

Colorado has more than one kind of court, and the right building depends on what you need to file or where your case is. This page shows how to use the official directory, how the courts are organized, and what to confirm by phone before you drive to a courthouse.

Use the official state court directory first. County and city courts usually run their own websites, and a search engine may send you to an old address or a paid directory that has nothing to do with the court.

Start with the official directory

Fastest route: open the Colorado judicial branch directory, choose your county, then confirm the court's address and phone number on the court's own page. If the county page is missing, call the state court administrator's office.

How Colorado's courts are organized

Colorado District Courts are the general trial courts. County Courts handle smaller civil and criminal cases, and Denver has a separate Probate Court.

Most states use some version of these levels. The names change from state to state, so do not assume that a "circuit court" in one state does the same work as a "circuit court" in another.

Which court handles which case

Court staff can tell you where a case is filed, but they usually cannot tell you which court is legally correct for your dispute. That is a legal question. The safest path is to check the court's own filing instructions, then confirm with the clerk. If you cannot afford a lawyer, a legal-aid office or court self-help center may review the question with you.

  • Criminal or traffic: the court listed on the citation or charging document.
  • Small claims: the limited-jurisdiction court for the county, when the amount is under its limit.
  • Probate: the court named in the Colorado probate guide.
  • Family: the family or general trial court for the county, often where the child or spouse lives.
  • Larger civil claims: the general trial court, usually in the county where the defendant lives or the dispute happened.

How to identify the correct county

People often confuse four different locations. They can point to different courthouses:

  1. Where a person lives. This matters for divorce, probate, and some family cases.
  2. Where an event occurred. A car crash or a slip-and-fall usually points to the county where it happened.
  3. Where property is located. A house, land, or a lien is usually handled where the property sits.
  4. Where a defendant may be sued. For a consumer or contract dispute, the defendant's county is often the starting point, but the rules can allow other choices.

If those four answers point to different counties, do not guess. Ask the clerk which filing the court accepts, or get brief advice from legal aid. Filing in the wrong county can cost you a filing fee and delay the case.

Verify before you travel

Courts move, merge, and change hours. A website address that worked last year may now be a closed satellite office. Call or check the court's own page for each item below.

Do not rely on a paid directory. Commercial court-locator sites often copy old addresses and add phone numbers for referral services. Use the official court site or call the state court administrator.

Call the clerk and ask

Keep the questions short and write down the answers, the date, and the name of the person you spoke with.

  • Is this the right court for my type of case?
  • Which county should the case be filed in?
  • What is the filing fee, and is there a fee waiver?
  • What forms do I need, and are they on the website?
  • Can I file in person, by mail, or online?
  • What are your hours and the last time you accept a filing?
  • Where do I park, and which entrance is accessible?
  • Do you offer interpreters or hearing assistance?
  • How do I find my case number after I file?

Common questions

How do I find the right courthouse in Colorado?

Start with the official Colorado judicial branch directory, choose the county, and confirm the address and phone number on the court's own page. If you are filing, call the clerk and describe the type of case before you travel.

Do I file where I live or where the defendant lives?

It depends on the case. For many civil disputes, the defendant's county or the county where the events happened is allowed. Probate usually follows the deceased person's county, and property cases often follow the property. Confirm with the clerk or legal aid.

Can I file by mail or online?

Often yes, but not for every case type. Ask the clerk whether your court accepts mail, e-filing, or drop-box filings, and whether an original signature is required.

What if I cannot afford the filing fee?

Most courts have a fee-waiver process. Ask the clerk for the waiver form and what proof of income is required. See our court fee waiver guide.
