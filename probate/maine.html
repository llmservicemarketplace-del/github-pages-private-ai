Maine Probate Records and Court Research: Official Free Resources

Last reviewed November 12, 2026. Written by The New Faith Church and Co-op, Inc. (NFCcop), a 501(c)(3) nonprofit educational resource. General information only, not legal advice. We sell no legal, search, background-check, or filing services.

If someone in Maine died and you need to find the estate file, start with the county, not with a search engine. Probate is a court process for sorting out what a person owned, what they owed, and who inherits. A judge or clerk supervises it. In most states, the file is held by the local court that handled the death, and only part of it ever appears online.

This page explains which court to call in Maine, what the records contain, how to ask for a copy, and when a small estate can skip full probate. It links only to official court and government pages and to free legal-aid programs. Some wills and probate files are available online. Many are not.

1. What probate is, in plain words

When a person dies, their money, house, car, and other property must be collected and given to the right people. Debts and taxes must be paid first. Probate is the court-supervised way to do that. The court confirms that the will is valid, appoints someone to manage the estate, and approves the final distribution.

Some property avoids probate. Life insurance with a named beneficiary, retirement accounts with a beneficiary, and property held with a right of survivorship usually pass directly. A house or bank account in the deceased person's name alone often does not.

2. Which court handles probate in Maine

Probate is handled by the Probate Court in the county where the person lived. The register of probate is the filing office.

Start here: find the Maine court that serves the county where the person lived at the time of death. The official state court site below lists courts, forms, and self-help contacts.

3. Find the right county and court

Probate is almost always filed where the deceased person lived, even if family members live somewhere else. Use these questions to narrow it down:

  • Where did the person live? This is usually the county of residence at death, even if they were in a hospital or care facility elsewhere.
  • Where is the property? A house in another county or state can create a second, separate proceeding. Ask the clerk about "ancillary" probate.
  • Was a case already opened? A will may have been filed earlier. Ask the clerk to search by the person's name and date of death, not just by case number.
  • Did the person live in another state? The home-state court usually starts the case. Maine may need to recognize that appointment before local property can be sold.

County lines and courthouse names can be confusing. Use the official Maine court directory and call the clerk before you mail anything.

4. Search for a probate case

The Maine court system publishes a public case lookup for some courts: state court case lookup. It may not include probate files, and some courts post only recent or limited dockets. Treat it as a starting point, not a complete index.

5. How to request a copy of a probate record in Maine

  1. Identify the county and the court. Use the directory above. Confirm the probate court's name, which can differ from the county's general courthouse.
  2. Gather the identifiers. Full legal name, any other names used, date of death, approximate filing year, and the case number if you have it.
  3. Ask what the clerk needs. Some courts require a written request or a specific form. Some accept email; many do not. Ask about certified copies versus plain copies.
  4. Ask about fees before ordering. Copy and certification fees are set locally and change. Ask for the current amount and how to pay.
  5. Keep the receipt and the envelope. If a copy is wrong or missing pages, the receipt makes a follow-up easier.
  6. Request an estimate if the file is large. Clerks can usually tell you the page count or the cost of a full copy.
Records are not all public. A probate file can contain medical information, account numbers, or sealed material. The clerk may deny or redact parts of a file. Ask what is available before you pay for a full copy.

6. Documents you will see in a probate file

Petition
The request that opens the estate. It names the deceased person, the heirs, and the person asking to be appointed.
Will
The deceased person's written instructions. The court decides whether it is valid. A will can be filed even when there is no property to distribute.
Letters testamentary
The court order giving the named executor authority to act. Banks and title companies usually ask for a certified copy.
Letters of administration
The same authority when there is no will, or when the named executor cannot serve. The court appoints an administrator.
Inventory
A list of what the estate owns and what it is worth, usually filed within a deadline set by local rules.
Final accounting
A summary of money received, bills paid, and what is left. Some states waive it for small or uncontested estates.
Closing order
The judge's order ending the case and confirming distribution. Ask the clerk for a certified copy if a bank or buyer needs proof.

7. Small estates and simplified probate in Maine

Maine offers simplified probate and a small-estate affidavit for estates under the statutory limit.

Every state sets its own dollar limit, waiting period, and forms. Those rules change, and a figure you find in an old blog post may be wrong. Check the current limit on the official court site or ask the probate clerk. Do not move or sell estate property based on a number you have not confirmed.

Real estate often follows separate rules. A small-estate affidavit may work for a bank account but not for a house. If the home was inherited without a clear deed, legal aid can help with title problems.

9. What to gather before searching

  • Full legal name, including maiden name, middle name, and any spelling used on court papers
  • Approximate date of death, and the state where it happened
  • County of residence at the time of death
  • Possible case number, if a bank, funeral home, or relative has a letter from the court
  • Name of the executor or administrator, if one was appointed

Also worth having: the last known address, the names of likely heirs, and a list of property in Maine. A clerk can search faster with a date and a county than with a name alone.

10. Common questions

Are all Maine wills online?

No. A will that has been filed may be part of the court file, but many courts do not post wills or full probate files online. Some counties publish scanned documents; others require a visit or a copy request. Never assume a will does not exist because a website did not show it.

Can I search probate records for free?

Often you can search an index for free and pay only for copies. Some courts charge for online access. Fees vary by county, so ask the clerk what the search and copy will cost before you order.

What if the person died in Maine but lived somewhere else?

The probate case usually starts where the person lived. If they owned real estate in Maine, a second local proceeding may be needed. Ask the clerk about ancillary probate and get advice if the property is valuable.

Do I need a lawyer to open an estate?

Not always. Simple estates are often handled without a lawyer. A contested will, a minor heir, a business, or a house with unclear title is a good reason to talk to legal aid or a lawyer before filing.
