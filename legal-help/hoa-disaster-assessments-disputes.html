HOA and Condo Special Assessments After a Disaster

Last reviewed November 10, 2026. Free public guidance from The New Faith Church and Co-op, Inc. (NFCcop), a 501(c)(3) nonprofit. This is general information, not legal advice. Rules differ by state and change over time, so confirm with the official sources below.

After a storm damages common areas or buildings, homeowners and condo associations sometimes send owners a large special assessment. That can happen when the association's insurance deductible is high or repairs aren't fully covered. You have rights to information, and your own insurance may help.

Check your loss assessment coverage

Many homeowners and condo (HO-6) policies include loss assessment coverage. It can pay your share of an assessment for a covered loss, such as storm damage, up to the limit on your policy. The default limit is often low, and higher limits are usually inexpensive to add before the next storm.

  • Send your insurer the assessment notice, the association's explanation of the loss, and the association's insurance information.
  • Coverage usually depends on the damage being from a cause your own policy covers.

Documents to request from the association

  • The board meeting minutes and vote that approved the assessment
  • Repair estimates and contracts
  • The association's insurance policy, deductible, and claim status
  • The budget, reserve study, and reserve balances
  • The governing documents (declaration, bylaws) showing the board's authority to assess

Most states give owners a right to inspect association records. Make your request in writing and keep a copy.

Questions to ask

QuestionWhy it matters
Did the board follow the notice and voting rules?Assessments that skip required steps may be challenged
Was an insurance claim filed, and what was paid?Insurance money should reduce what owners pay
Are reserves being used where allowed?Reserves may cover some repairs
How was each owner's share calculated?It should follow the governing documents

If you can't pay

  • Ask the board about a payment plan in writing.
  • Don't ignore it. Unpaid assessments can lead to fees, liens, and in some cases foreclosure.
  • Ask whether the association is applying for loans or grants that could spread the cost.

If you think it's improper

Raise it in writing, attend board meetings, and talk with other owners. Your state may have an agency or ombudsman for condo and HOA issues; Florida has one for condominiums. For serious disputes, talk to a lawyer who handles community association law. See free legal help.

Frequently asked questions

Does homeowners insurance cover an HOA special assessment?

Often, through loss assessment coverage, if the assessment is for damage from a cause your policy covers. Check your limit.

Can my HOA charge a special assessment after a hurricane?

Usually yes, if the governing documents and state law allow it and the board follows required procedures.

Can I see the HOA's records?

Most states give owners a right to inspect association records. Make the request in writing.
