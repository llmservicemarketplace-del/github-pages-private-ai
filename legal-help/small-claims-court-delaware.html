Small Claims Court in Delaware: Limit, Court, and How to File

Last reviewed November 14, 2026. Written by The New Faith Church and Co-op, Inc. (NFCcop), a 501(c)(3) nonprofit educational resource. General information only, not legal advice. We are not a government agency and we charge nothing for these guides.

Short answer: In Delaware, small claims cases are heard in Justice of the Peace Court. The limit is $25,000.

Delaware has no separate small claims court. The Justice of the Peace Court is where people file smaller civil claims without a lawyer.

Small claims court is the simplified part of the civil court for lower-dollar disputes: an unreturned security deposit, an unpaid loan, a repair that was not done, damage to your car or property. The forms are short and you can file without a lawyer. This page covers the limit and the court in Delaware, how to file, and what happens after.

Before you file

  1. Send a demand letter. Say what you are owed, why, and give a date. Many cases end here, and some courts expect it. See how to write a demand letter.
  2. Check the deadline. Every type of claim has a statute of limitations. See civil lawsuit deadlines.
  3. Get the defendant's legal name. For a business, that is the registered name, which may differ from the sign on the door.
  4. Ask whether you can collect. A judgment against someone with no income or assets may never be paid.

Step by step: filing small claims in Delaware

  1. Get the forms. Start with Courts: start a civil action in the Justice of the Peace Court, or ask the clerk of Justice of the Peace Court.
  2. Fill in the claim. State the amount and, in a few plain sentences, why the defendant owes it.
  3. File and pay the fee. If you cannot afford it, ask for a waiver. See court fee waivers.
  4. Serve the defendant. The court tells you which methods count. You usually cannot hand over the papers yourself. See serving court papers.
  5. Prepare your evidence. Bring the contract or receipt, photos, messages, estimates, and a one-page timeline. Bring copies for the judge and the other side.
  6. Go to the hearing. Arrive early. Be brief and stick to dates, amounts, and documents. See preparing for a court hearing.
  7. Get the judgment. Ask the clerk how and when it is issued and whether either side can appeal.

If you win: collecting

Winning gives you a judgment, not a check. If the other side does not pay, you use the court's collection tools. See how to collect a judgment.

If you are the one being sued

Do not ignore the papers. If you miss the response date or the hearing, the court can enter a default judgment for the full amount. See avoiding a default judgment and sued by a debt collector.

If your claim is over the limit

You can usually either give up the amount over the limit and stay in small claims, or file a regular civil case. See filing a civil lawsuit in Delaware. For a dispute with an insurer, read suing your insurance company without a lawyer and consider an insurance complaint in Delaware first.

Delaware details

Justice of the Peace Court handles most small cases

Delaware's Justice of the Peace Court hears civil claims up to $25,000, plus landlord-tenant cases. The courts explain how to start a case and how to appeal.

Common questions

What is the small claims court limit in Delaware?

The limit is $25,000. Delaware has no separate small claims court. The Justice of the Peace Court is where people file smaller civil claims without a lawyer. Limits change, so confirm the figure with the court before you file.

Which court hears small claims in Delaware?

Justice of the Peace Court. You usually file in the county where the defendant lives or where the dispute happened.

Do I need a lawyer for small claims court in Delaware?

No. Small claims is built for people to represent themselves. Whether a lawyer is allowed to appear at all varies by state, so check the court's small claims page.

What if I win and the other side does not pay?

The court does not collect for you. You may need to use tools such as wage garnishment or a bank levy, which have their own forms and fees.
