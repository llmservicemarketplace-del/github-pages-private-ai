What to Do If You Get an Eviction Notice

Last reviewed November 8, 2026. General legal information from The New Faith Church and Co-op, Inc. (NFCcop), a 501(c)(3) nonprofit. Laws vary by state; this is not legal advice.

A notice from your landlord to pay or leave is usually the first step, not the eviction itself. In nearly every state, a landlord must go through court before you can be removed. A landlord generally cannot lock you out, remove your things, or shut off utilities to force you out.

Read the notice carefully

  • What kind of notice is it? Pay rent or quit, fix a lease violation, or end of lease.
  • What is the deadline? Write it down.
  • Is it court papers? A summons and complaint from a court has a short deadline to respond. Missing it can mean a default judgment against you.

What to do now

  1. Call legal aid immediately. Many areas have free eviction defense programs.
  2. Look for rental assistance if the problem is unpaid rent. Call 211.
  3. Respond to court papers by the deadline, even if you're working on a deal with the landlord. See avoiding a default judgment.
  4. Keep paying what you can and get receipts, unless a lawyer advises otherwise.
  5. Gather records: lease, rent receipts, texts, repair requests, photos.
  6. Go to every court date.

Living in a home that was foreclosed? Tenants often have extra protections. Repairs not done? See when a landlord won't make repairs.

Free help

Contact legal aid as early as you can, especially if there is a court deadline. Find your local program through our legal aid guide, LawHelp.org, or by calling 211.

Questions this guide answers

Can my landlord lock me out without going to court?

In nearly every state, no. A landlord generally must get a court order before removing a tenant, and lockouts and utility shutoffs to force you out are illegal.

What happens if I ignore eviction court papers?

The court can enter a default judgment against you, which can lead to removal from the home. Respond by the deadline and call legal aid.
