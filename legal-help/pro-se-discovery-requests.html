Discovery Basics When You Represent Yourself

Last reviewed November 10, 2026. Free public guidance from The New Faith Church and Co-op, Inc. (NFCcop), a 501(c)(3) nonprofit. This is general information, not legal advice. Rules differ by state and change over time, so confirm with the official sources below.

Discovery is the part of a lawsuit where each side gets information and documents from the other. If you're representing yourself (pro se), you have the same discovery rights as a lawyer, and the same duties to follow the rules. This guide covers the basics. Your court's rules control, so read them for your exact court.

The main tools

ToolWhat it isFederal court basics
InterrogatoriesWritten questions the other side must answer in writing, under oathUp to 25, including subparts, unless the court allows more (Rule 33)
Requests for productionRequests for documents, emails, photos, or other itemsDescribe each item or category with reasonable detail (Rule 34)
Requests for admissionStatements the other side must admit or denyNot answered on time can be treated as admitted (Rule 36)
DepositionsQuestioning a witness under oath, recorded by a court reporterUsually costs money for the reporter and transcript (Rule 30)

Answers are generally due 30 days after service in federal court. State courts set their own limits and deadlines. For example, Georgia and Florida state courts use different interrogatory limits than federal court. Check your state's rules of civil procedure.

Before you send discovery

  • In federal court, formal discovery usually can't start until the parties have held their planning conference (Rule 26(f)), unless the court orders otherwise.
  • Check the court's scheduling order for the discovery deadline. Requests must be served early enough that answers are due before it.
  • Discovery requests are usually served on the other side, not filed with the court.

Writing good requests

  • Number each request and keep each one focused on one subject.
  • Tie requests to the claims and defenses in the case. Discovery must be relevant and proportional.
  • Define key terms once, such as "the Property" or "the Claim."
  • Use requests for admission to lock in facts that shouldn't be in dispute, like the authenticity of a document.

When you receive discovery

  • Calendar the deadline the day you receive it.
  • Answer honestly and completely, or state a specific objection. General objections are often not accepted.
  • If you need more time, ask the other side in writing for an extension, and confirm any agreement in writing.

If the other side won't answer

  1. Send a letter or email identifying the missing answers and asking them to fix it by a date.
  2. Try to talk it through. Most courts require a good-faith attempt to resolve the dispute before filing a motion.
  3. If that fails, you can file a motion to compel (Rule 37 in federal court), describing your attempt to resolve it.

Many courts have self-help centers and free legal clinics for pro se litigants. See preparing for mediation and free legal help.

Frequently asked questions

How many interrogatories can I send?

In federal court, 25 including subparts unless the court allows more. State courts set their own limits, so check your state's rules.

What happens if someone doesn't answer requests for admission?

In federal court, requests for admission not answered or objected to within 30 days can be treated as admitted. State rules are often similar.

Do I file discovery with the court?

Usually not. Discovery is served on the other party. You file it only when needed, such as with a motion.
