What to Do If a Debt Collector Sues You

Last reviewed November 8, 2026. General legal information from The New Faith Church and Co-op, Inc. (NFCcop), a 501(c)(3) nonprofit. Laws vary by state; this is not legal advice.

Debt collectors sue millions of people a year, and many cases end in a default judgment because the person never responded. Responding gives you a chance to make the collector prove the debt, raise defenses, or negotiate.

Steps

  1. Find the deadline on the summons to file an answer or appear, and calendar it. Deadlines are often short.
  2. Confirm it's real. Check the court's records or call the clerk. Scammers sometimes send fake papers.
  3. File a written answer by the deadline. Court self-help centers often have forms. See avoiding a default judgment.
  4. Raise defenses that fit: it's not your debt, wrong amount, already paid, or too old to sue on.
  5. Go to every hearing and bring your records.

Know your protections

  • Time-barred debt: debt collectors are not allowed to sue or threaten to sue on debts past the state's time limit.
  • Federal benefits such as Social Security, SSI, and VA benefits are generally protected. See wage garnishment and bank levies.
  • You can still negotiate a settlement. Get any agreement in writing and filed with the court.

Free help

Contact legal aid as early as you can, especially if there is a court deadline. Find your local program through our legal aid guide, LawHelp.org, or by calling 211.

Questions this guide answers

What happens if I ignore a debt collection lawsuit?

The court will likely enter a default judgment, which can lead to wage garnishment or bank levies.

Can a debt collector sue me for an old debt?

Federal rules prohibit debt collectors from suing or threatening to sue on time-barred debt, meaning debt past your state's time limit.
