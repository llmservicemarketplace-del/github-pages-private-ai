How to Collect a Judgment You Won

Last reviewed November 8, 2026. General legal information from The New Faith Church and Co-op, Inc. (NFCcop), a 501(c)(3) nonprofit. Laws vary by state; this is not legal advice.

A judgment is the court's decision that someone owes you money. The court usually won't collect it for you. If the other side doesn't pay voluntarily, you must use collection tools the law allows.

Steps

  1. Ask for payment in writing once the time to appeal has passed. Offer a payment plan if it helps.
  2. Find assets. Many courts let you require the debtor to answer questions under oath about income and property (a debtor's examination).
  3. Use collection tools your state allows:
    • Wage garnishment through the debtor's employer.
    • Bank account levy.
    • Judgment lien on real estate, recorded with the county.
  4. Track payments and file a satisfaction of judgment when paid in full. Many states require it.
Some income is protected from collection, such as most Social Security benefits and a portion of wages. See wage garnishment limits.

Judgments generally last for years and can often be renewed. Check your state's rules.

Free help

Contact legal aid as early as you can, especially if there is a court deadline. Find your local program through our legal aid guide, LawHelp.org, or by calling 211.

Questions this guide answers

Will the court collect my judgment for me?

Usually not. You must use collection tools such as wage garnishment, bank levies, or liens.

How long does a judgment last?

It depends on the state, often many years, and many states let you renew it.
