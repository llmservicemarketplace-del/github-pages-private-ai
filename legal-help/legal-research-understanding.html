Legal Research and Understanding Legal Terms

Legal research is the process of identifying and reviewing the authoritative laws, judicial opinions, and procedural rules that govern a specific question. Legal English, statutory cross-references, and procedural terms can make research challenging. Understanding how to locate primary legal sources and distinguish credible authorities from promotional commentary gives you greater confidence when evaluating your legal options.

Primary Sources vs. Secondary Sources

Effective legal research starts by recognizing the hierarchy of legal authorities:

  • Primary Legal Sources: The binding laws established by government authorities. These include constitutions, statutes enacted by legislatures, published court decisions (case law), administrative regulations, and court procedural rules. Accessible public repositories include the Legal Information Institute (LII) at Cornell Law School and research guides from the Library of Congress Law Library.
  • Secondary Sources: Materials that explain, analyze, or summarize primary law. Examples include legal dictionaries, law school treatises, bar journal articles, practice guides, and legal encyclopedias. Secondary sources help you understand an issue, but they are not binding law.

Navigating Ambiguous Terms and Legal English

Search phrases in legal research often combine technical, colloquial, or branded terminology whose meanings depend on context:

Context-Dependent Phrases Explained:

  • Legal Power: In legal theory and doctrine, "power" generally refers to an individual's or institution’s recognized legal capacity to create or alter legal relationships (such as authority granted under a power of attorney, or an agency's regulatory authority). It is a legally defined capacity, not an abstract or informal concept.
  • Human Law: In legal philosophy and jurisprudence, this phrase often contrasts positive law established by human legislative and judicial institutions with moral or natural law theories. Its meaning depends on the academic or philosophical context.
  • "Family in Law" vs. Familial Status: While everyday language refers to in-laws through marriage, civil law evaluates specific statutory definitions of spouses, registered domestic partners, legal dependents, and heirs when determining rights in inheritance, healthcare decisions, and wrongful death claims.
  • Live Law: The phrase "Live Law" commonly refers to named legal journalism and case-reporting services (such as the prominent legal news platform LiveLaw) rather than a generic legal doctrine.
  • Visual Law: A modern discipline that applies graphic design, visual timelines, and clear formatting to help make dense legal contracts, regulations, and trial exhibits easier to understand.

How to Verify Whether Legal Information Is Current

Laws and regulations are regularly amended, updated, or repealed. A court opinion issued years ago may have been overturned by an appellate court or modified by statute. When conducting research:

  • Check the effective date and amendment history of statutes on official legislative portals.
  • Confirm whether court opinions remain good law and have not been vacated, overruled, or superseded.
  • Verify administrative regulations through official compilations, such as the Electronic Code of Federal Regulations (eCFR) in the United States.

Evaluating Law Firm Directories and "Top 100" Lists

Search results frequently feature queries like "top 100 law firms" or commercial attorney rankings. Many online directories and "super lawyer" lists are published by commercial media organizations or reflect peer nominations, marketing submissions, or paid advertising. A ranking list does not replace verifying:

  • Whether the attorney holds an active license in good standing with the local bar or regulator.
  • Direct experience in the specific factual and legal subject of your case.
  • Fee transparency, direct client reviews, and local court familiarity.
