What to Do About a Mechanic's Lien on Your Home

Last reviewed November 8, 2026. General legal information from The New Faith Church and Co-op, Inc. (NFCcop), a 501(c)(3) nonprofit. Laws vary by state; this is not legal advice.

A mechanic's lien (construction lien) lets a contractor, subcontractor, or supplier who wasn't paid for work on your property file a claim against the property itself. A lien can block a sale or refinance, and in some cases can lead to foreclosure. Lien rules and deadlines are set by state law.

How to protect yourself

  • Use a written contract and pay as work is completed, not far in advance.
  • Collect lien waivers from the contractor, subcontractors, and suppliers with each payment.
  • Watch for any preliminary notice from subcontractors or suppliers. Many states require them.
  • Pay suppliers jointly with the contractor if they ask for it.

If a lien is filed

  1. Get a copy from the county recorder's office.
  2. Check the deadlines: liens usually must be filed, and lawsuits to enforce them started, within strict time limits. A lien that misses them may be removable.
  3. Contact the person who filed it. If you already paid, send proof.
  4. Ask about a lien release once the matter is paid or resolved.
  5. Talk to a lawyer. Some states let you bond off a lien or ask a court to remove an invalid one.

Free help

Contact legal aid as early as you can, especially if there is a court deadline. Find your local program through our legal aid guide, LawHelp.org, or by calling 211.

Questions this guide answers

Can a subcontractor put a lien on my house if I paid my contractor?

In many states, yes, if the contractor didn't pay them. Lien waivers and joint checks help prevent this.

How do I remove a mechanic's lien?

Pay or resolve the debt and get a recorded release, or challenge an invalid or expired lien through the process your state allows. A lawyer can help.
