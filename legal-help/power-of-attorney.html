Financial Power of Attorney Basics

Last reviewed November 8, 2026. General legal information from The New Faith Church and Co-op, Inc. (NFCcop), a 501(c)(3) nonprofit. Laws vary by state; this is not legal advice.

A financial power of attorney (POA) is a document that lets you name someone you trust, your agent, to handle money and legal matters for you. A durable POA keeps working if you become unable to make decisions.

Types

  • Durable: stays in effect if you become incapacitated.
  • Springing: starts only when a specified event happens, such as a doctor certifying incapacity.
  • Limited: covers one task, such as selling a house.
  • Health care decisions use a separate document. See sharing health information with family.

Making it valid

Each state sets its own rules for signing, notarizing, and witnesses. Many states have a statutory form. Legal aid and elder law programs often help for free.

Choosing and protecting

  • Pick someone trustworthy and organized. You can name a backup.
  • Agents must act in your interest, keep your money separate, and keep records.
  • You can revoke a POA in writing while you have capacity.
  • Suspect financial abuse? Contact Adult Protective Services or the police.

Free help

Contact legal aid as early as you can, especially if there is a court deadline. Find your local program through our legal aid guide, LawHelp.org, or by calling 211.

Questions this guide answers

What is a durable power of attorney?

A power of attorney that stays in effect if you become unable to make decisions yourself.

Does a power of attorney need to be notarized?

Many states require notarization and sometimes witnesses. Check your state's rules or use its statutory form.
