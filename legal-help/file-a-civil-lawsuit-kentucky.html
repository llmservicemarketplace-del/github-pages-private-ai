How to File a Civil Lawsuit in Kentucky: Courts, Forms, Fees, and Service

Last reviewed November 12, 2026. Written by The New Faith Church and Co-op, Inc. (NFCcop), a 501(c)(3) nonprofit educational resource. General information only, not legal advice. We sell no legal, search, background-check, or filing services.

A civil lawsuit is how one person or business asks a court to settle a dispute with another. It is not a criminal case, and no one goes to jail because of it. The person who files is the plaintiff; the person being sued is the defendant.

This guide walks through the filing sequence in Kentucky: choosing the court, preparing the complaint, paying or waiving the fee, serving the defendant, and tracking the case. It is general information, not legal advice, and it does not promise any result.

Civil cases and criminal cases are different

  • Criminal case: a government prosecutes a person for breaking a criminal law. The punishment can include jail, fines, or both.
  • Civil case: a private party sues another private party, or sometimes a government agency, over money, property, a contract, or harm. The remedy is usually money or an order to do or stop something.

The same event can lead to both. A car crash can produce a traffic charge and a civil claim for repairs or medical bills. They are separate cases with separate rules.

Regular civil court and small claims

Small-claims court is a simplified part of the civil court for lower-dollar disputes. The forms are shorter, the hearings are informal, and lawyers are often not required. The dollar limit and the types of cases allowed vary by state.

If your claim is small, read our small claims guide before you file a regular civil case. Filing in the wrong track can waste a fee. If your claim is large or complicated, small claims may not be available.

Jurisdiction and venue, in plain language

Jurisdiction asks whether a court has the power to hear this kind of case and this person. Venue asks which county or district is the proper location. A court can have jurisdiction but still be the wrong venue.

Civil cases are filed in Circuit Court, or in District Court for smaller claims.

For a consumer, contract, or personal-injury dispute, the answer often depends on where the defendant lives, where the agreement was made, or where the harm happened. Do not assume that you can file in your own county just because it is convenient.

Step by step: filing a civil lawsuit in Kentucky

  1. Identify the legal claim. Write down what happened, what rule or agreement was broken, and what remedy you want. "They were unfair" is not a legal claim; "they broke a written contract and owe $X" is closer.
  2. Check the deadline. Statutes of limitation set how long you have. They differ by claim type. Missing the deadline can end the case, so check before you spend money on filing.
  3. Choose the correct court and county. Use the Kentucky courthouse guide and confirm with the clerk.
  4. Prepare the complaint. Most courts publish a form or a required format. State the facts in order, identify the parties, and say what you want the court to order.
  5. Prepare a summons if the court requires one. The clerk usually issues or stamps it. Do not invent a court form.
  6. Pay the filing fee or request a waiver. Ask for the current fee and the fee-waiver form at the same time.
  7. File with the clerk. Keep stamped copies of everything. Ask how the court assigns the case number.
  8. Arrange legally valid service. The defendant must receive the complaint and summons in the way the rules allow. In most states, a party to the case cannot do this personally.
  9. Keep proof of service. File the affidavit or certificate with the court. Without it, the case may not move forward.
  10. Track deadlines and hearings. Put every date on a calendar, check the docket, and respond to motions. A missed deadline can cost you the case.

Deadlines: there is no single answer

Kentucky sets different limitation periods for contracts, property damage, personal injury, debt, and other claims. The clock can start on different dates, and some claims have special notice requirements. This page does not give a universal deadline because a wrong number is worse than no number.

Check the official statute or the court's own guidance, and ask legal aid if you are close to a deadline. Our civil lawsuit deadlines guide explains how to find the rule that applies. Federal claims have their own limits and should be checked separately.

Filing fees and fee waivers

Filing fees depend on the court, the case type, and the amount in dispute. A fee schedule can change without much notice. Ask the clerk for the current amount for your exact case, and ask whether other costs apply, such as service, copies, or a jury fee.

If you cannot afford the fee, ask for a fee waiver or deferral. Most courts require a form listing income, expenses, and sometimes assets. A waiver can cover the filing fee but usually not the cost of serving the defendant. See our court fee waiver guide.

Serving the defendant in Kentucky

Service is the formal delivery of the complaint and summons. It is not a text, an email, or a social-media message unless the rules specifically allow it. The rules usually require a sheriff, a process server, or another adult who is not a party to the case.

Do not serve the papers yourself if the state's rules prohibit it. Serving your own case can be treated as improper service and can get the case dismissed. Keep the original affidavit of service and file it with the court.

Read our guide to serving court papers and the court's own service rules. If the defendant cannot be found, ask the clerk about alternative service; do not simply skip service.

Filing online and finding your case afterward

Some Kentucky courts accept electronic filing for self-represented parties; others limit it to attorneys or certain case types. Ask before you rely on a portal.

After filing, ask the clerk for the case number and the judge or division. Check the docket before each hearing, and keep your contact information current with the court.

Before filing: what to gather

  • Full legal names and current addresses of every party
  • A plain timeline of what happened, with dates
  • Contracts, leases, invoices, or written agreements
  • Photos, videos, and screenshots with dates
  • Receipts, bank records, and estimates for repairs or losses
  • Emails, letters, and text messages, saved in full
  • A written calculation of the money you are asking for
  • Any notices, demand letters, or termination letters

Organize the documents before you go to the clerk. A short index with numbered exhibits makes the filing easier to review and helps you at a hearing.

Common questions

How much does it cost to file a civil lawsuit in Kentucky?

It depends on the court and the case type. Filing fees, service costs, and copy fees are separate. Ask the clerk for the current fee for your exact case and whether a fee waiver is available.

Which court do I file in?

Usually the general trial court for the county where the defendant lives or where the dispute happened, or a limited-jurisdiction court if the amount is small. The correct answer depends on the claim, so confirm with the clerk or legal aid before filing.

Can I serve the defendant myself?

Usually not. Most states require service by a sheriff, a process server, or an adult who is not a party to the case. Follow the court's rules and file proof of service.

How long do I have to sue?

It depends on the type of claim. Kentucky sets different limits for contracts, injury, property, and debt. Check the official statute or ask legal aid; do not rely on a general number you find online.
