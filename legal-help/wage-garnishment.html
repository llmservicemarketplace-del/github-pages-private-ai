Wage Garnishment and Bank Levies: What Can Be Taken

Last reviewed November 8, 2026. General legal information from The New Faith Church and Co-op, Inc. (NFCcop), a 501(c)(3) nonprofit. Laws vary by state; this is not legal advice.

After a creditor wins a court judgment, it may be able to garnish your wages or levy your bank account. Federal law sets limits, and many states protect more.

Federal wage garnishment limits (ordinary debts)

For most consumer debts, the most that can be garnished each week is the lesser of:

  • 25% of your disposable earnings, or
  • the amount by which your weekly disposable earnings exceed 30 times the federal minimum wage. At $7.25 an hour, that is $217.50 a week. If you take home $217.50 or less a week, nothing can be garnished for ordinary debts.
Debt typeFederal limit
Child support / alimonyUp to 50–65% of disposable earnings
Defaulted federal student loans and other federal non-tax debtsUp to 15%
Federal and state taxesOrdinary limits don't apply

Your employer can't fire you because your pay is garnished for any one debt.

Federal benefits in your bank account

If Social Security, SSI, VA benefits, federal retirement, or certain other federal payments are directly deposited, your bank must protect up to two months of those benefits automatically. Benefits received by paper check don't get that automatic protection.

Claim your exemptions

States may protect more income and property. If you get a garnishment or levy notice, look for the exemption claim form and file it by the deadline. Legal aid can help.

Free help

Contact legal aid as early as you can, especially if there is a court deadline. Find your local program through our legal aid guide, LawHelp.org, or by calling 211.

Questions this guide answers

How much of my paycheck can be garnished?

For most consumer debts, federal law limits garnishment to the lesser of 25% of disposable earnings or the amount above 30 times the federal minimum wage per week. Some states protect more.

Can a debt collector take my Social Security?

Generally no for ordinary debts. If benefits are directly deposited, your bank must protect up to two months of them. Exceptions include back taxes, federal student loans, and child support.
