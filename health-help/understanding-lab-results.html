Understanding Your Lab Results

Last reviewed November 9, 2026. Free public guidance from The New Faith Church and Co-op, Inc. (NFCcop), a 501(c)(3) nonprofit. This is general information, not legal or medical advice. Rules can change, so confirm with the official sources below.

Lab reports are full of numbers, abbreviations, and flags. Here’s how to read them, and why a result outside the range isn’t always a problem.

Parts of a lab report

PartWhat it means
Test nameOften an abbreviation, such as CBC (complete blood count) or A1C.
ResultYour number or finding.
UnitsHow it was measured, such as mg/dL. Different labs may use different units.
Reference rangeThe range seen in most healthy people at that lab. Ranges vary by lab, age, and sex.
FlagH (high), L (low), or A (abnormal) when a result is outside the range.
Collection dateWhen the sample was taken. Use it to compare results over time.

Reading your results

  • Slightly out of range is common. About 1 in 20 healthy people will fall outside a normal range on any single test.
  • Trends matter. A change over several tests often means more than one number.
  • Your situation matters. Medicines, recent meals, exercise, or pregnancy can change results.

Why you may see results first

Federal rules now generally require that you can get your electronic health information, including lab results, without unnecessary delay. That means results may appear in your patient portal before your doctor has reviewed them. It’s okay to wait for your doctor’s explanation. You can also get your results directly from the lab that ran the test.

Call your doctor promptly if a result is marked critical or you have new symptoms. Don’t change medicines based on a result without talking to your care team.

Track results over time with PAi Health, which places lab reports in a timeline on your own PC.

How to read your blood test report

Most lab reports list each test in a row with these parts:

ColumnWhat it means
Test nameWhat was measured, such as glucose, hemoglobin, or LDL cholesterol. Names are often abbreviated (for example, WBC = white blood cells).
ResultYour number.
UnitsHow it was measured, such as mg/dL or g/dL. Compare results only when units match.
Reference rangeThe range seen in most healthy people at that lab. Ranges differ between labs and can depend on age and sex.
Flag"H" (high) or "L" (low) if your result is outside the range.

A result slightly outside the range is common and often not a problem. About 1 in 20 healthy people fall outside a typical range on any single test. What matters is the whole picture: your symptoms, other results, medicines, and how results change over time.

Common blood tests explained

  • CBC (complete blood count): red blood cells, hemoglobin, and hematocrit (oxygen-carrying, low can mean anemia), white blood cells (infection-fighting), and platelets (clotting).
  • BMP or CMP (metabolic panel): glucose (blood sugar), electrolytes like sodium and potassium, kidney tests (BUN, creatinine, eGFR), and on the CMP, liver tests (ALT, AST, alkaline phosphatase, bilirubin) and protein.
  • Lipid panel: total cholesterol, LDL ("bad"), HDL ("good"), and triglycerides.
  • Hemoglobin A1C: average blood sugar over about 3 months. Below 5.7% is normal, 5.7% to 6.4% suggests prediabetes, and 6.5% or higher suggests diabetes.
  • Fasting glucose: under 100 mg/dL is normal; 100 to 125 suggests prediabetes; 126 or higher on two tests suggests diabetes.

Results in your patient portal before your doctor calls

Federal rules mean many results now appear in your online portal as soon as they're ready, sometimes before your doctor has reviewed them. Seeing a flag can be stressful. It doesn't always mean something is wrong.

When to call your doctor

  • Your doctor's office hasn't contacted you within the time they said, and you have questions.
  • A result is flagged as critical or the lab or office calls you about it.
  • You have new or worsening symptoms. If you have chest pain, trouble breathing, or signs of a stroke, call 911.

Write down questions to ask: What does this result mean for me? Does it need to be repeated? Is any change in medicine or follow-up needed? For plain-language explanations of specific tests, see MedlinePlus Lab Tests, from the National Library of Medicine.

This guide is general information, not medical advice. Your doctor is the best person to explain what your results mean for you.

Frequently asked questions

What does H or L mean on lab results?

H means the result is higher than the lab's reference range and L means lower. Being slightly out of range isn't always a problem. Ask your doctor what it means for you.

Why are lab ranges different between labs?

Each lab sets its own reference ranges based on its equipment and methods. Always compare a result with the range printed on the same report.

Can I see my lab results before my doctor does?

Often, yes. Federal rules generally require that you can get your electronic health information without unnecessary delay, so results may appear in your portal before your doctor reviews them.
