Your Right to Access Your Medical Records

Last reviewed November 8, 2026. Free public guidance from The New Faith Church and Co-op, Inc. (NFCcop), a 501(c)(3) nonprofit. This is general information, not legal or medical advice. Rules can change, so confirm with the official sources below.

Under the federal privacy law known as HIPAA, you have the right to see and get a copy of your health information held by doctors, hospitals, clinics, pharmacies, and health plans. That includes your medical records and your billing records. A provider cannot refuse to give you your records because you have an unpaid bill.

Steps to request your records

  1. Find the right office. Call the hospital or clinic and ask for Health Information Management (HIM) or the Medical Records department. Many providers also let you download records from a patient portal.
  2. Put your request in writing. Providers may require a written request, and many have their own form. They can verify who you are, but they cannot make the process unreasonably hard.
  3. Be specific. List your full name, date of birth, the dates of service, which records you want (for example, “complete medical record including imaging reports and lab results”), and the format you want: paper, electronic file, email, or portal.
  4. Ask about cost up front. Ask for a fee estimate before they make the copies.
  5. Keep proof. Keep a copy of your request and write down the date you sent it and who you spoke to.

Sample request letter

Copy, fill in, sign, and date this if the provider does not have its own form:

To: Health Information Management / Medical Records [Provider name and address] I am requesting a copy of my protected health information under 45 CFR 164.524 (HIPAA Right of Access). Name: [your full name] Date of birth: [date] Dates of service: [from] to [to] Records requested: [complete medical record / specific records] Format: [electronic copy by email or portal / paper copy by mail] Send to: [your address or email] Please tell me in advance if there will be a fee. Signature: ______________________ Date: __________

How long they have and what they can charge

DeadlineGenerally within 30 calendar days of your request. A provider can extend this once, by up to 30 more days, only if it tells you in writing why and when you will get the records. Some state laws set a shorter deadline. HHS has said it plans to propose changes to this deadline, so check the official sources below for the current rule.
FeesOnly a reasonable, cost-based fee for copying labor, supplies, and postage. Providers cannot charge you for searching for or retrieving your records. For electronic copies of electronic records, a provider may instead choose to charge a flat fee of no more than $6.50.
FormatYou can ask for the form and format you want, such as an electronic file, if the provider can readily produce it.
Unpaid billsNot a valid reason to deny or delay your request.

What you may not get

  • Psychotherapy notes that a therapist keeps separate from your main record.
  • Information put together for a lawsuit or legal proceeding.

If a provider denies any part of your request, it must tell you why in writing. For some kinds of denials, you can ask for the decision to be reviewed by another licensed health professional.

Sending records to someone else

You can ask a provider to send your records directly to another person you choose, such as a new doctor, a lawyer, or an insurance adjuster. Make this request in writing and say clearly who should receive the records and where to send them. Fee rules can be different when records go to someone other than you, so ask about cost first.

If your request is ignored or refused: send a second written request that cites 45 CFR 164.524 and gives a date you expect a reply. If that doesn’t work, you can file a complaint with the HHS Office for Civil Rights, generally within 180 days of when you knew of the problem.

Why your full record matters after a disaster

Complete records help with insurance claims for injuries, FEMA medical and funeral expense help, disability applications, and replacing prescriptions lost in a storm. Request them early, before you need them.

Frequently asked questions

Can a hospital refuse to give me my records because I owe money?

No. HHS guidance says a provider may not withhold or deny your records because of an unpaid bill. It may charge a reasonable, cost-based fee for the copies.

How long does a provider have to give me my medical records?

Generally 30 calendar days, with one possible extension of up to 30 more days if the provider tells you in writing why. Some states require faster responses.

How much can they charge for my medical records?

Only a reasonable, cost-based fee for copying labor, supplies, and postage. For electronic copies of electronic records, a provider may instead charge a flat fee of no more than $6.50.
