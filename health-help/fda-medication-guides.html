Decoding FDA Medication Guides

Last reviewed November 8, 2026. Free public guidance from The New Faith Church and Co-op, Inc. (NFCcop), a 501(c)(3) nonprofit. This is general information, not legal or medical advice. Rules can change, so confirm with the official sources below.

Some prescription medicines carry serious risks that patients need to understand. For these drugs, the U.S. Food and Drug Administration (FDA) requires a Medication Guide: an FDA-approved handout written in plain language. Your pharmacy must give it to you on paper each time the drug is dispensed. You can ask for an electronic copy instead.

Do not stop or change a medicine on your own because of something you read in a Medication Guide. Talk with your doctor or pharmacist first. For a medical emergency, call 911. For a possible overdose or poisoning, call Poison Help at 1-800-222-1222.

How a Medication Guide is organized

Federal rules set the same headings for every Medication Guide, so once you know the layout, you can find answers fast:

  1. What is the most important information I should know about this drug? The most serious risks, including any boxed warning.
  2. What is this drug? What it treats.
  3. Who should not take this drug? Conditions, allergies, or situations that make it unsafe for you.
  4. How should I take this drug? Dosing directions and what to do if you miss a dose.
  5. What should I avoid while taking this drug? Such as alcohol, certain foods, driving, or other medicines.
  6. What are the possible side effects? Including which ones need medical attention right away.
  7. General information about safe use, including storage, and the date the guide was last revised.

What to look for

  • Serious side effects: warning signs that mean you should call your doctor or get emergency care.
  • Reasons not to take it: health conditions, pregnancy, or other medicines that make the drug unsafe for you. Tell your doctor and pharmacist about every medicine and supplement you take.
  • Things to avoid: alcohol, driving, or other activities while taking the drug.
  • Storage and disposal: how to keep the medicine safe from children and pets, and how to get rid of unused medicine.

How to find a Medication Guide

  • Ask your pharmacist. You are entitled to the guide with each fill.
  • Search the FDA’s Medication Guides list, linked below.
  • Look up the full drug label on DailyMed from the National Library of Medicine.

Getting rid of unused medicine

The best option is a drug take-back site or a pharmacy disposal kiosk. If none is available, the FDA explains which medicines to flush and how to throw others away safely in household trash.

Report a side effect

Tell your doctor about any side effect. You can also report it to the FDA through MedWatch, online or by calling 1-800-FDA-1088.

After a disaster

If your medicines were lost or damaged in a storm, ask a pharmacy for an emergency refill and a new Medication Guide. Never take pills that got wet or were stored in extreme heat without checking with a pharmacist first.

Track your medicines in one private place

PAi Health checks the medication names in your records against FDA labels for interactions, warnings, and duplicate ingredients, all on your own computer. It gives label-based context, not medical advice. Read the PAi Health privacy guide or ask NFCcop for free access.

Frequently asked questions

What is an FDA Medication Guide?

An FDA-approved, plain-language handout required for certain prescription drugs with serious risks. Pharmacies must give it to you with the medicine.

Is the pharmacy printout the same as a Medication Guide?

No. Most pharmacy information sheets are not reviewed or approved by the FDA. A Medication Guide is FDA-approved and required only for specific drugs.

Where can I find the Medication Guide for my medicine?

Ask your pharmacist, search the FDA Medication Guides list, or look up the full label on DailyMed.
